\section{Introduction}

% Par 1
Sensory modalities (e.g., vision, olfaction, hearing, echolocation) differ greatly in what they allow an animal to perceive about its environment. Each modality has its own profile of which environmental features it encodes \citep[e.g.,][]{Boonman2013}. Bat echolocation, for example, carries the distance to the nearest reflecting surface faithfully \citep{Kothari2018}, the spatial layout of complex scenes only lossily \citep{Vanderelst2016}, and colour not at all. Vision has a different profile: it carries colour and the environment's two-dimensional layout faithfully, but distance only lossily, recoverable only approximately from cues such as disparity, motion parallax, and occlusion.

Despite their differences, different modalities can carry information about the same feature. Their access to the world overlaps, allowing animals to access world features through more than one sensory chain. Bottlenose dolphins recognise object features across echolocation and vision \citep{Herman1998,Harley2003}; for a review, see \citet{Bruck2022}. Rodents access spatial and shape features of nearby objects through both whiskers and vision \citep{Winters2010,Nikbakht2018,Guyoton2025}. Crucially, when modalities carry the same feature, they can differ in how accessible it is and how much computational effort is needed to recover and represent this feature. For example, for vision, the azimuth and elevation of objects is easility recoverable because of the retinoptopical encoding. In contrast, the azimuth and elevation of a sound source requires computation to recover. 

The function that recovers a feature from a modality's sensory data is often called the modality's \emph{inverse model}; in animals, it is what the modality's neural pathways implement. In bats, for instance, the auditory pathway and cortical circuits filter and transform raw echo data to explicitly represent the distance to the nearest object \citep{Kothari2018,Suga1990}. For some (modality, feature) pairs the inverse is trivial, or at least more straightforward. Vision's retinotopic encoding makes object azimuth directly available: the mapping from cortical location to direction in space is built into the topography of the visual pathway. For other pairs the inverse must be learned. The binaural signal carries the azimuth of a sound source, but its extraction requires neural circuitry that compares interaural time and intensity differences \citep{Grothe2003}.

The overlap and an asymmetry in feature encoding across modalities gives rise to a learning opportunity. If one modality already extracts a given feature, it can supply training labels for another modality whose chain carries the same feature but does not yet extract it. Barn owls are a well-known example. In these birds, vision calibrates the auditory map of space, and prisms that displace the visual map induce a matching displacement of the auditory map \citep{Knudsen1995}. The auditory pathway already carries the spatial information; what vision supplies is the supervisory signal that says which auditory pattern should mean what location in the world. Once trained, the auditory map runs on its own, and the owl can hunt in darkness. The same kind of cross-modal calibration is documented in mammals: the auditory space map in ferret superior colliculus develops normally only when matched visual experience is available \citep{KingCarlile1995}, and adult primate auditory localization can be recalibrated by sustained audio-visual spatial conflict \citep{Recanzone1998}. Most documented cases of cross-modal calibration involve vision in the teaching role, perhaps reflecting vision's high spatial resolution and broad feature coverage \citep{MuganMacIver2020}; the framework, however, applies wherever the asymmetry of inverse difficulty holds.

This framework may have particular relevance for bats. Echolocating bats operate in highly cluttered, spatially demanding environments: they navigate dense vegetation, return to specific roosts and feeding sites, and follow stable flight routes between them \citep{Holderied2006,Schnitzler2003,Barchi2013}. Yet sonar is a poor instrument for recovering the structure of complex scenes. Natural objects return cascades of overlapping echoes \citep{Yovel2009,Vanderelst2016}, and the bat cochlea integrates these into responses that no longer resolve the individual reflectors \citep{Wiegrebe1996,Wiegrebe2008}. Behavioural and neural evidence both indicate that closely-spaced objects in cluttered scenes are perceived as single extended stimuli \citep{Geberl2019,Warnecke2018}. The information needed to reconstruct a 3D scene from sonar alone is, for natural environments, largely absent. Yet bats display remarkable spatial competence: stable idiosyncratic flight paths in dark cages \citep{Barchi2013}, centimetre-precision memory for feeder locations \citep{VonHelversen2005}, and successful homing after displacement \citep{Stones1969}.

Bats also see. Most echolocating species have functional vision \citep{Boonman2013}, and the cross-modal calibration mechanism described above suggests how vision could supply the spatial labels that sonar cannot easily produce on its own. A bat with visual access to an environment can build a representation from which spatial features are derivable for any pose, and that representation can both train the sonar pathway to extract the same features from echoes and drive offline rehearsal of behaviours that will later be deployed by sonar alone. We call this \emph{cross-modal vicarious learning}: training a behaviour through one modality and deploying it through another. The bat that visually surveyed a space at dusk can, in principle, navigate it in total darkness.

The present paper instantiates this idea on a physical robot. The robot carries onboard sonar in a bat-inspired configuration (two receivers flanking an emitter) and is observed externally by an overhead camera paired with an annotated arena map. Distances to the nearest walls in three azimuthal slices are computable for any pose by direct geometric construction from the map; these geometric labels supervise the training of an inverse model from sonar envelope to the same three slice distances. The scene model also supplies imagined slice distances at arbitrary poses along a desired path, paired with the actions of a path-following teacher, to train a navigation policy. At deployment, the overhead camera and the map are out of the loop, and the robot follows a path in an arena it has only seen, not pinged.

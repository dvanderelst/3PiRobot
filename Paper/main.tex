\section{Introduction}

% Par 1
Sensory modalities (e.g., vision, olfaction, hearing, echolocation) differ greatly in what they allow an animal to perceive about its environment. Each modality encodes different environmental features \citep[e.g.,][]{Boonman2013,Page2021}. Despite their differences, their access to the world overlaps, allowing animals to encode world features through more than one sensory chain. Bottlenose dolphins, for example, recognise object features across echolocation and vision \citep{Herman1998,Harley2003,Bruck2022}. Rodents access spatial and shape features of nearby objects through both whiskers and vision \citep{Winters2010,Nikbakht2018,Guyoton2025}. Crucially, when modalities carry the same feature, they can differ in how accessible it is and how much computational effort is needed to recover and represent it. Bat echolocation, for example, carries the distance to the nearest reflecting surface faithfully \citep{Kothari2018}, the spatial layout of complex scenes only lossily \citep{Vanderelst2016}, and colour not at all. Vision, in contrast, makes the azimuth and elevation of objects easily recoverable because of the retinotopical encoding. Distance, on the other hand, is recoverable only approximately from cues such as disparity, motion parallax, and occlusion. Likewise, in hearing, the azimuth and elevation of a sound source require computation to recover.

% Par 2
The function or mapping that recovers world features from a modality's sensory data is often called the modality's \emph{inverse model}. This term is used more generally for the recovery of latent quantities from observed signals, in fields such as sensorimotor control \citep{Jordan1992,Wolpert1998}, model-based reinforcement learning \citep{Sutton1990,Ha2018}, and predictive coding \citep{Rao1999,Friston2010}. In animals, it is what the modality's neural pathways implement to turn sensation into perception and representation. The complexity of this inverse depends on the specific combination of modality and feature, not on the modality alone. For some combinations the inverse is trivial, or at least directly available from the modality's encoding. In bats, for instance, the auditory pathway and cortical circuits filter and transform raw echo data to explicitly represent the distance to the nearest object, which is a fairly straightforward operation \citep{Peremans2012,Wiegrebe2008}. Similarly, vision's retinotopic organisation makes object azimuth directly readable from the location of activity in the visual pathway \citep{Wandell2007,Szeliski2022}. For other combinations the inverse must be learned. The binaural signal carries source azimuth, but its extraction requires neural circuitry that compares interaural time and level differences \citep{Grothe2003}. Similarly, vision carries information about depth, but its extraction requires specialised circuitry that combines binocular disparity, motion parallax, and related cues \citep{Cumming2001,Parker2007}. The same modality can therefore appear on both sides of the asymmetry, depending on which feature is considered.

% Par 3
The overlap, but asymmetry, in feature encoding across modalities gives rise to a learning opportunity. If one modality already extracts a given feature, it can supply training labels for another modality that carries the same feature but does not yet extract and represent it. Barn owls are a well-known example. In these birds, vision calibrates the auditory map of space, and prisms that displace the visual map induce a matching displacement of the auditory map \citep{Knudsen1995}. The auditory pathway carries the spatial information (i.e., the azimuth and elevation of a sound source) but the inverse model is lacking. What vision supplies is the supervisory signal that labels auditory patterns with world locations, allowing the auditory inverse model to be learned. Once trained, the auditory pathway can run the inverse model on its own, mapping sound to space, and the owl can hunt in darkness. The same kind of cross-modal calibration is documented in mammals. The auditory space map in ferret superior colliculus develops normally only when matched visual experience is available \citep{King1995}, and adult primate auditory localisation can be recalibrated by sustained audio-visual spatial conflict \citep{Recanzone1998}. Outside mammals and birds, weakly electric fish trained on visual landmarks can later recognise the same landmarks electrosensorily \citep{Schumacher2017}. Interestingly, most documented cases of cross-modal calibration involve vision in the teaching role, perhaps reflecting vision's high spatial resolution and broad feature coverage \citep{Mugan2020}. We refer to this general mechanism as \emph{cross-modal inverse training}, which applies wherever the asymmetry of inverse difficulty holds.

% Par 4
Bats provide a particularly interesting case for cross-modal inverse training. They have two sensory modalities, vision and echolocation, that both carry information about space but operate under sharply different environmental constraints \citep[e.g.,][]{Holland2005,Page2021}. Echolocating bats operate in highly cluttered, spatially demanding environments: they navigate dense vegetation, return to specific roosts and feeding sites, and follow stable flight routes between them \citep{Holderied2006,Schnitzler2003,Barchi2013}. While echolocation is sometimes described as ``seeing with sound'' \citep[e.g.,][]{Moss2001,Simmons2012,Ulanovsky2008,Clare2015,Geipel2013}, it likely does not yield the rich, image-like information this metaphor implies. Indeed, for reasons we explained in detail elsewhere \citep[e.g.,][]{Vanderelst2015,Vanderelst2016,Steckel2013,Mansour2019,Mansour2019a}, sonar is poor at recovering the structure and layout of complex scenes. Natural objects return cascades of overlapping echoes \citep{Yovel2009,Vanderelst2016}, and the bat cochlea integrates these into responses that no longer resolve the individual reflectors \citep{Wiegrebe1996,Wiegrebe2008}. Behavioural and neural evidence both indicate that closely-spaced objects in cluttered scenes are perceived as single extended stimuli \citep{Geberl2019,Warnecke2018}. It follows that the information needed to reconstruct a 3D scene from sonar alone is, for natural environments, largely absent. Yet bats display remarkable spatial sonar-based competence, including stable idiosyncratic flight paths in dark cages \citep{Barchi2013}, centimetre-precision memory for feeder locations \citep{VonHelversen2005}, and successful homing after displacement \citep{Stones1969}. Hence, while spatial information is not readility available through sonar, it carries some spatial features that are recoverable provided the inverse function can be learned.

% Par 5
Echolocating bats also see. Most echolocating species have functional vision \citep{Boonman2013,Hoffmann2019}, with behaviourally measured visual acuity in microchiropterans roughly comparable to that of similarly sized small mammals such as rats and deer mice \citep{Manske1976,Bell1986,Cechetto2020}. Their luminance sensitivity is within an order of magnitude of that of mice, sufficient for navigation under starlight \citep{Cechetto2023}. Therefore, the cross-modal calibration mechanism described above suggests how vision could supply spatial labels for learning an inverse model for specific features carried by but not easily extracted from sonar. A bat could learn this inverse model while having access to both vision and sonar, e.g., while exploring environments not in complete darkness. Indeed, many bats emerge from their roosts at dusk and forage under intermediate light levels of 1--10\,lux \citep{Boonman2013}, echolocate even in bright light \citep{Holland2005,Eitan2022}, modify their echolocation in response to available visual cues \citep{McGowan2020}, and preferentially recruit visual cues for prey detection when these are available \citep{Eklof2003,Holland2009}. Early field experiments established that vision contributes to long-distance homing in bats \citep[e.g.,][]{Mueller1957,Williams1966a,Williams1966b,Williams1967,Williams1970}.

% Par 6
The overlap in feature encoding across modalities also gives rise to a second opportunity. Sensory modalities differ not only in feature accessibility but also in how richly they support internal modelling of the environment. Vision is particularly well suited to this role. Its long operating range gives advance information about distal events, a property argued to have underwritten the evolution of planning and prospective cognition in land vertebrates \citep{Mugan2020,Bennett2023}. % Par 7
A bat with a visual representation of an environment and an inverse model for features available to both sonar and vision could engage in offline rehearsal of behaviours that will later be deployed by sonar alone. In other words, an inverse model for features shared across sonar and vision could support \emph{cross-modal vicarious learning}, i.e., training a behaviour through one modality and deploying it through another. \cnote{5}{We should probably add some more arguments for the existence of internal models supported by vision.}

% Par 8
Cross-modal vicarious learning would specifically benefit sensory modalities with low informational throughput that are ill-suited to build a coherent environmental representation, such as whisking, sonar, or tactile sensing. Effectively, the representation built using the richer sensory modality (e.g., vision) can be used to train the animal in its use of the more proximate sense.

% Par 9
This paper instantiates two forms of cross-modal learning in a bat-like robot, direct learning and vicarious learning. In each case, an inverse model is first trained from echoes using locally available labels supplied by an overhead camera that acts as a proxy for depth vision; the trained inverse then drives behaviour in a downstream task in which the robot acts on sonar alone.
% LEAVE THIS HERE TO HAVE A VISUAL MARKER OF PARAGRAPH STRUCT
First, we demonstrate cross-modal direct learning. The inverse model for this demonstration is trained on echoes collected in a single arena. The labels locate the nearest reflector: its class (wall or cardboard pole), a three-direction depth profile when the reflector is a wall, and the pole's azimuth when the reflector is a pole. Direct learning does not require this inverse to generalise beyond the training environment, only to recover the locally relevant features reliably within it. We then mimic the layout of the Holland-style perch-finding paradigm \citep{Holland2005,Finger2025}: the robot is released in an arena with surrounding walls and a central cardboard pole. A simple reactive rule on the inverse model's output (approach the pole, avoid the wall) is sufficient for the task. We hand-code this rule as a stand-in for the trial-by-trial association a bat would acquire when reinforced to find the perch. At test, the rule and inverse run on sonar alone, with vision unavailable.
% LEAVE THIS HERE TO HAVE A VISUAL MARKER OF PARAGRAPH STRUCT
Second, we demonstrate cross-modal vicarious learning. Assuming the bat has a visual model of the environment, it can use offline training to perform a spatial task in an environment it has not explored acoustically. The inverse model for this demonstration is therefore trained on echoes collected across a range of arena layouts, again using overhead-camera-derived labels, so that it can support sonar reconstruction of environments the agent has not yet encountered acoustically. We demonstrate this by training the robot to follow a prespecified path through the environment. The asymmetry in inverse-model scope between the two demonstrations mirrors the demands of the two operations. Direct learning calls for a specialised inverse fitted to the operational environment, whereas vicarious learning calls for a general inverse capable of supporting offline rehearsal in counterfactual settings.



% Par 10

In this paper, we do not claim to model any specific bat experimental finding. Instead, this paper presents a robotic demonstration of cross-modal inverse training and direct learning in echolocating bats, for which there is some evidence (see discussion). In addition, we also demonstrate how this, in turn, could support vicarious learning. Note that our robotic model operates in biologically plausible conditions in terms off environmental complexity and sensing noise. Moreover, its sonar system is limited compared biosonar of bats. We a narrowband emitter (as opposed to the broadband emssions typically used by bats in dense environments) which produces long pulses (as opossed to the very brief pulses bats would use under these conditions) at a fairly low carrier freqency (as opposed to the higher frequencies bats typocally use). Hence, successfully demonstrating these mechanisms under such constraints establishes that they are feasible for bats in principle, without claiming that bats actually employ them.


\section{Methods}

\subsection{Arena and Robot}

% Par 11
The experiments reported below shared a common physical setup: a small wheeled robot operating inside a walled arena, under an overhead camera system that tracked its pose. The arena was approximately 3.5 by 3.5 m. The backing of the walls was formed by cardboard panels, each about 80 cm wide and about 30 cm high. Onto the cardboard, we glued foam tiles sold as decorative background for terraria. These panels had a variety of surface textures simulating different types of natural rock. The tiles were fixed to the cardboard with 5 cm gaps between them. The gaps and the textured tile surfaces together produced complex, overlapping reflections.

The arena walls were light and could easily be repositioned so the arena layout could be reconfigured rapidly between data-collection runs. Sub-assemblies built from four panels fixed at right angles were also used as freestanding rectangular obstacles inside the arena. In addition to the panel walls and panel sub-assemblies, we used vertical poles as a second class of in-arena obstacle. Each pole was a wooden dowel of 25 mm diameter and approximately 60 cm height, held upright by a low-profile metal base so that it stood unaided on the floor. Poles could be placed anywhere within the arena. The arena floor consisted of interlocking high-density foam tiles with a smooth top surface, chosen to give the wheels reliable traction and to provide a uniform background contrast for the overhead tracker.

% Par 14
The robot was a Pololu 3pi+~2040 (Pololu Robotics \& Electronics, Las Vegas, NV), a commercially available differential-drive platform built around an RP2040 microcontroller (diameter: 90 mm). We added a HiLetgo ESP8266 ES Wi-Fi module so that all higher-level control logic could run off-board on a host computer, while the robot executed low-level motion and acoustic commands on request. Communication with the host was by JSON-framed messages over a TCP link.

% Par 15
Three MaxBotix MB1360 XL-MaxSonar-AEL0 ultrasonic transducers (MaxBotix Inc., Brainerd, MN) were mounted on the robot in a 3D-printed housing. Two transducers acted as left and right ``ears'': their centres sat 37 mm apart and each was angled outward by 15$^{\circ}$ in azimuth, so that their main axes diverged by 30$^{\circ}$. The third transducer was mounted on the midline, 15 mm below the ears, and pointed straight ahead; it acted as the emitter, or ``mouth''. Emissions were tone bursts at a carrier frequency of 42 kHz and a duration of approximately 2.5 ms. Each ear recorded the resulting echoes as the demodulated envelope, sampled by the robot's analogue-to-digital converters at 10 kHz for 200 samples per ping, i.e., a 20 ms acquisition window per ear.

% Par 16
The robot's pose in the arena was read out from an overhead camera system. Two ceiling-mounted cameras covered complementary halves of the arena with a strip of overlap near the centre. The robot carried a unique ArUco fiducial marker on its top plate, which the cameras detected frame by frame. Per-camera intrinsics were calibrated from images of a circular-dot calibration target; a homography was then fitted from a single image of a board placed on the floor, which fixed the world origin, the in-plane axis directions and the floor-plane pixel-to-millimetre scale for each camera. The two camera frames were aligned by measuring the in-plane offset between their floor-plane origins, so that one camera acted as the reference frame and the other's detections were translated into it by a fixed displacement. The output of this pipeline, queried at run time over a TCP link, was the robot's floor-plane position ($x$, $y$, in millimetres) and yaw angle, in a single shared coordinate frame across both cameras. The same coordinate frame was used by every component of the system, including the sonar-training labels, the trained policies, and the trajectory analyses presented later. The overhead camera system was also used to digitise the layout of the arena for each experiment.

We then annotated each per-camera view by hand: green polylines traced the top edge of every panel wall, and blue marks indicated the top of every pole. Because each annotated pixel originated from a camera with a known pose, we back-projected it as a ray from the camera centre through the corresponding world point, and intersected that ray with the horizontal plane at the known feature height. Annotating each camera's view separately, rather than annotating the stitched composite, avoided parallax artefacts: tall features such as poles cast slightly different apparent positions in the two camera views, and back-projecting each view through the correct feature height recovered the base coordinates without the averaging ghost that the stitched image carried. 


\section{Discussion}

% Terminology used throughout the Discussion:
%   cross-modal inverse training — the learning event: acquisition of
%     an inverse model in the receiver modality using feature labels
%     supplied by the supervisor modality. Conditions 1-3.
%   cross-modal direct learning — the agent learns or performs a task
%     in real time with the receiver modality engaging the environment,
%     relying on the cross-modally trained inverse. No internal model
%     of the environment required. Conditions 1-5 (= training + 4, 5).
%   cross-modal vicarious learning — direct learning's conditions plus
%     offline mental rehearsal of task behaviour against an internal
%     representation. Conditions 1-7 (= direct + 6, 7).

% Par 18
For cross-modal direct learning of a given task to be observed in an experiment, several conditions need to be jointly satisfied. They fall into three tiers: the framework's mechanism, the experimental conditions for observing it, and the additional conditions specific to vicarious learning beyond plain cross-modal direct learning. The framework's central operation, cross-modal inverse training, requires:
%
\begin{itemize}
\item[1] A task-relevant feature must be physically encoded in the sensory data of both modalities and be recoverable in principle.
\item[2] One of the two modalities, the supervisor, must already extract this feature explicitly and represent it, so that it can supply training labels.
\item[3] The other modality, the receiver, must have acquired an inverse model that recovers the feature from its raw sensory data using the supervisor's labels. This acquisition can happen developmentally through natural bimodal experience or during the training phase of an experiment.
\end{itemize}

% Par 19
Cross-modal inverse training (conditions 1-3) is not in itself observable in an experiment. For cross-modal direct learning to be observed, two further conditions must be satisfied:
%
\begin{itemize}
\item[4] The task must hinge on the cross-modally accessible feature, not on some other feature that is accessible only in the supervisor modality.
\item[5] The animal must engage the receiver-side inverse at test rather than bypass it via a different strategy.
\end{itemize}

% Par 20
Cross-modal direct learning (conditions 1-5) is the observable deployment of a cross-modally trained inverse: the receiver modality engages the environment in real time, and the inverse makes the cross-modally accessible features available for task learning or performance. Cross-modal \emph{vicarious} learning of task behaviour additionally requires:
%
\begin{itemize}
\item[6] The agent must hold an internal representation of the required task features. This representation can be modality-specific or amodal.
\item[7] The agent must engage in mental rehearsal of the task against this representation, generating imagined input for the receiver modality at arbitrary states of the world.
\end{itemize}

% Par 21
For bats, the inverse model for features encoded in both vision and sonar can be acquired at two distinct times, with different consequences for what an experiment can show. First, an inverse can be learned developmentally, through cumulative bimodal experience of natural environments. Bats often use sonar under conditions where vision is also available, and these instances provide the supervised cross-modal pairs the framework requires. The inverses formed this way are general and ecologically relevant. They cover features the animal regularly encounters across many environments, such as the presence and approximate distance of nearby obstacles, the coarse spatial layout of cluttered scenes, and the presence of familiar landmark types. Once developmentally acquired, these inverses are available to the bat in any subsequent task that probes the same features.

% Par 22
Alternatively, an inverse can be acquired during the experiment itself, when the task hinges on features that are specific to the experimental setup, idiosyncratic, or otherwise not covered by the bat's developmental inverses. Finding a particular perch placed in an unfamiliar room is one such case; discriminating between two laboratory-fabricated targets is another. For these tasks, the framework's conditions for cross-modal inverse training must be satisfied within the experiment itself. Vision and sonar must be co-available and consistently paired during the training phase so that vision's labels can supervise the sonar-side inverse. If the experimental design suppresses or limits this co-availability, the new inverse cannot form.

% Par 23
The two regimes carry different experimental signatures and call for different interpretations of negative results. Under the first regime, an experiment polls inverses the animal already possesses, and a failure to observe cross-modal performance suggests either that the animal did not deploy the inverse it has, or that the specific experimental setup falls outside the distribution the developmental inverse was trained on. Under the second regime, the experiment tests whether a new inverse can be acquired under the conditions provided, and a failure can reflect a violation of any of criteria 1 to 5. The bar an experiment must clear is therefore much higher under the second regime than under the first.

% Par 24
This distinction has a corollary for experimental design. The more ecologically remote the task, the more its features fall outside any developmentally-formed inverse, and the more the experiment must itself do the work of establishing the new inverse. Tasks built around laboratory-fabricated targets, geometric discriminations bats would never encounter in the wild, or arbitrary perch-in-room mappings all sit in the second regime by construction. The probability of observing cross-modal direct learning in such tasks decreases accordingly, not because cross-modal direct learning is absent in bats but because the experiment is asking the bat to acquire a new inverse from scratch under whatever cross-modal supervision the design provides. Ecologically natural tasks, by contrast, can lean on inverses the animal has already formed; the experiment then needs only to probe whether the animal deploys them.

% Par 25
The joint criteria are stringent. They are especially demanding in experiments where the bat has access to both modalities during training and is free to learn the task using any feature it finds, since the animal might converge on a strategy that does not engage the cross-modal route. Despite its potential adaptive value, convincing evidence for cross-modal direct learning in bats is lacking \citep[see also][]{Page2021}.

% Par 26
\citet{Danilovich2019} report two experiments testing cross-modal direct learning using \animal{ra}. In the first experiment, bats learned to discriminate two shapes while having access to both vision and sonar. Next, they tested whether the bats could discriminate the objects using sonar alone. They found that the bats were unable to discriminate between the objects in the dark.
This failure to demonstrate cross-modal direct learning might have been due to several criteria not being satisfied. First, with additional training in darkness, only one out of four bats was eventually able to complete the task using sonar. This indicates that the task might have been hard to complete acoustically, perhaps because task-relevant features cross-modally encoded at sufficient fidelity were not available (criterion 1), preventing the bats from learning or using an inverse model (criterion 3). In addition, during training with vision available, bats might have learned to use features of the objects that are not cross-modally available, leading to a failure during the testing phase when those features were removed (criterion 5).

% Par 27
In a second experiment, \citet{Danilovich2019} trained \animal{ra} to discriminate between two textures using sonar. Next, they tested whether the bats could perform the task using vision. In a cleaner replication of the experiment, only one out of five bats managed this \citep[Round 2 in][]{Danilovich2019}. This limited cross-modal performance could again be explained by a failure to satisfy the criteria outlined above. Even if bats were able to perceive task-relevant features using both sonar and vision (criteria 1-3), they might have learned to use these, except for the successful individual, during the training (criterion 5).

% Par 28
Similar limitations make findings of \citet{Holland2005} inconclusive. These authors trained \animal{ra} to find a hidden perch in light with sonar masked by ultrasonic noise, then tested in darkness with echolocation alone. Performance dropped and bats had to relearn finding the perch, which they were able to do. The authors interpreted the need to relearn as evidence of no transfer between the two sensory systems. However, as for the experiments reported by \citet{Danilovich2019}, experimental evidence supporting criterion 1 is lacking. More fundamentally, the masking-noise design invalidates criterion 3. With sonar suppressed during training, the supervised cross-modal pair the framework requires cannot form, and any task-specific inverse model for finding the perch in this room had no opportunity to be acquired. Pre-existing inverses developed through natural bimodal experience might have remained available, but they would not address the specific perch-in-this-room mapping the task probes.

% Par 29
Even positive evidence needs to be scrutinised. \citet{Finger2025} trained one group of \animal{ra} to land on a fixed perch in light and a second only in darkness. The light-trained group later landed in darkness, while the dark-only group failed across many trials, with three of four bats never contacting the perch at all. \citet{Finger2025} interpret these findings as evidence for some form of cross-modal direct learning since bats that were first trained using sonar and vision managed to later find the perch using sonar alone while the sonar-only group did not. However, the two of three pretrained bats required substantial additional training when restricted to only using sonar. Hence, in these animals cross-modal direct learning must have been limited.

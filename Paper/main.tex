\section{Introduction}

% Par 1
Sensory modalities differ greatly in what they allow an animal to perceive about its environment, and which environmental features they encode \citep[e.g.,][]{Boonman2013,Page2021}. However, modalities also partially overlap in their environmental access, enabling animals to access features of the world through multiple sensory systems. Bottlenose dolphins, for example, recognize object features across echolocation and vision \citep{Herman1998,Harley2003,Bruck2022}. Rodents access spatial and shape features of nearby objects through both whiskers and vision \citep{Winters2010,Nikbakht2018,Guyoton2025}.

% Par 2



The function or mapping that recovers world features from a modality’s sensory data is often called the modality’s \emph{inverse model}. This term is used more generally to refer to the recovery of latent quantities from observed signals, in fields such as sensorimotor control \citep{Jordan1992,Wolpert1998}, model-based reinforcement learning \citep{Sutton1990,Ha2018}, and predictive coding \citep{Rao1999,Friston2010}. In sensory systems, it is what the modality’s neural pathways implement to turn sensation into perception and representation. The complexity of this inverse depends on the specific combination of modality and feature, not on the modality alone. Indeed, even when modalities can access the same environmental features, they can differ substantially in how accessible they are and how much computational effort is required to recover and represent them. For example, vision’s retinotopic organization makes object azimuth directly readable from the location of activity in the visual pathway \citep{Wandell2007,Szeliski2022}. For hearing, the inverse is less straightforward and must be learned. The binaural signal carries information about source azimuth, but extracting this information requires neural circuitry that compares interaural time and level differences \citep{Grothe2003}.

% Par 3
The overlap in feature encoding but asymmetry in ease of recovery across modalities presents a learning opportunity. If one modality already extracts a given feature, it can supply training labels for another modality that carries the same feature but does not yet extract and represent it. In other words, a modality that recovers a feature readily can supervise another in learning an inverse model for that same feature, a process we term \emph{cross-modal inverse training}. Barn owls are a well-known example of vision supporting the learning of an inverse model for audition. In these birds, vision calibrates the auditory map of space, and prisms that displace the visual map induce a matching displacement of the auditory map \citep{Knudsen1995}. In this example, the auditory pathway carries spatial information (i.e., the azimuth and elevation of a sound source), but the inverse model is missing. What vision supplies is the supervisory signal that labels auditory patterns with world locations, allowing the auditory inverse model to be learned. Once trained, the auditory pathway can run the inverse model on its own, mapping sound to space, and the owl can hunt in darkness. The same kind of cross-modal inverse training is documented in mammals. The auditory space map in ferret superior colliculus develops normally only when matched visual experience is available \citep{King1995}, and adult primate auditory localization can be recalibrated by sustained audiovisual spatial conflict \citep{Recanzone1998}. Outside mammals and birds, weakly electric fish trained on visual landmarks can later recognize the same landmarks electrosensorily \citep{Schumacher2017}. Note that most of these examples share a common form. What the supervisor modality provides is a correspondence between two spatial maps of the same two variables, i.e., the azimuth and elevation of a source, so that the receiver's map can be brought into register with the supervisor's. The inverse we consider below is not of this kind. It is object-based rather than map-based, in the sense that it must first determine what kind of reflector the sensory data came from and only then recover the geometry appropriate to that kind. Cross-modal correspondences at the level of objects rather than of low-level perceptual features are documented in humans, where they engage anterior temporal structures distinct from the posterior regions that bind perceptual features across modalities \citep{Taylor2006}. Learning an inverse of this sort is therefore likely to be more demanding than aligning two maps of the same space.

% Par 4
Echolocating bats provide an interesting opportunity for cross-modal inverse training. They have two sensory modalities, vision and echolocation, that both encode information about space and object features. However, the two modalities differ greatly in how easily those features can be accessed \citep[e.g.,][]{Holland2005,Page2021}. Echolocation allows bats to operate in highly cluttered, spatially demanding environments. Besides foraging on the wing, it enables them to navigate dense vegetation, return to specific roosts and feeding sites, and follow stable flight routes between them \citep{Holderied2006,Schnitzler2003,Barchi2013}. The auditory pathway and cortical circuits filter and transform raw echo data to explicitly represent the distance to the nearest object, a fairly straightforward operation based on the time of return \citep{Peremans2012,Wiegrebe2008}. Recovering features beyond this distance, however, requires substantial computation even when the relevant information is present in the echoes \citep{Baier2019}. While echolocation is sometimes described as ``seeing with sound'' \citep[e.g.,][]{Moss2001,Simmons2012,Ulanovsky2008,Clare2015,Geipel2013}, it likely does not support reconstructing the rich, image-like information the metaphor implies, for reasons we have detailed elsewhere \citep[e.g.,][]{Vanderelst2015,Vanderelst2016,Steckel2013,Mansour2019,Mansour2019a} and that are consistent with behavioral and neural evidence \citep{Geberl2019,Warnecke2018}. Natural objects and scenes return cascades of overlapping echoes \citep[e.g.,][]{Yovel2009,Vanderelst2016}, and the bat cochlea integrates these into responses that no longer resolve the individual reflectors \citep{Wiegrebe1996,Wiegrebe2008,Warnecke2018}.  Attempts in machine learning to reconstruct 3D models from sonar have likewise had limited success, even for relatively simple indoor environments \citep{Brunetto2023,Zhang2025,Christensen2020a,Frank2020a,Parida2021}. Any inverse model that bats might learn for sonar is therefore most likely dedicated to recovering specific action-relevant features rather than a generic 3D reconstruction of the environment.

% Par 5
Echolocating bats also see and have functional vision \citep{Boonman2013,Hoffmann2019}. \citet{Bell1986} measured minimum visible angles of 0.06 to 1 degree across three insectivorous bat species, retained down to starlight intensities, while \citet{Cechetto2020} report a spatial resolution of 0.6 cycles per degree in \animal{md}. These acuities are comparable to those of similarly sized rodents \citep{DeSousa2022} and are mostly insufficient for hunting insects but should support spatial orientation \citep{Cechetto2023}. For comparison, normal human vision has an acuity of 30 cycles per degree \citep{DeSousa2022}.

Vision can also recover the distance to objects, not only their direction. Many bats have frontally directed eyes with appreciable binocular overlap: in the gleaning bat \animal{mc} the binocular field spans about 50 degrees, roughly twice that of two aerial-hawking insectivores examined alongside it \citep{Bell1986}, and a temporal retinal area of high ganglion-cell density, typical of binocular vision, is present in both echolocating and non-echolocating species \citep{Cechetto2020}. Binocular overlap is the classical substrate for stereoscopic distance perception \citep{Cumming2001,Parker2007}. Moreover, even where binocular overlap is limited, monocular cues, in particular the motion parallax and optic flow generated by the bat's own movement, could provide distance information \citep{Kugler2019}.

% Par 6
The cross-modal mechanism introduced above could therefore operate in echolocating bats, with vision providing the labels for learning such a feature-based inverse, recovering features that are carried by, but not easily extracted from, sonar. A bat could acquire this inverse whenever it has access to both senses, for example when exploring environments that are not completely dark. Indeed, many bats emerge from their roosts at dusk and forage under intermediate light levels of 1--10\,lux \citep{Boonman2013}, and echolocate even in bright light \citep{Holland2005,Eitan2022}. Which features can be recovered, and how reliably, probably depends on the environmental conditions under which the inverse is deployed.

Early field experiments established that vision contributes to long-distance homing in bats \citep[e.g.,][]{Mueller1957,Williams1966a,Williams1966b,Williams1967,Williams1970}.

% Par 7
Here, we demonstrate the feasibility of cross-modal inverse training in a bat-like robot and show that the trained inverse model, although noisy, is sufficient to support behavior. First, we train an inverse model from echoes using locally available labels from an overhead camera, which serves as a proxy for vision. This inverse model recovers a limited set of features, including the class of the nearest reflector (wall or pole) and a very coarse depth profile when the reflector is a wall, and the pole’s azimuth when the reflector is a pole. Results indicate that recovering these features from sonar is not trivial, and the resulting inverse model is noisy. Despite this, we show that it allows completing two tasks using sonar alone. The first task is an obstacle-avoidance and target-approach task. The second task is following a preset path through the environment, integrating path integration with recognition of landmarks identified by the inverse model. Previous work \citep{Yovel2009,Yovel2011,Wang2022}, including our own \citep{Vanderelst2016,Achutha2021} has shown that sonar can be used to classify objects and scenes. However, the current paper extends this work by showing that the inverse model is sufficiently reliable to control behavior.

% Par 8
By using a small mobile robot equipped with a bat-like sonar system, this paper shows that the acquisition of an inverse model and its use in controlling behavior is biologically plausible. Indeed, the robotic model operates in environments that return real, complex echoes. Moreover, the robot's sonar is more limited than the biosonar of bats. We use a narrowband emitter (as opposed to the broadband emissions typically used by bats in dense environments) which produces long pulses (as opposed to the very brief pulses bats would use under these conditions) at a fairly low carrier frequency (as opposed to the higher frequencies bats typically use). Hence, successfully demonstrating these mechanisms under such constraints establishes that they are feasible for bats in principle.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Arena and Robot}

% Par 11
The experiments reported below shared a common physical setup: a small wheeled robot operating inside a walled arena, under an overhead camera system that tracked its pose (Fig. \ref{fig_robot}x). The arena was approximately 3.5 by 3.5 m. The backing of the walls was formed by cardboard panels, each about 80 cm wide and about 30 cm high. Onto the cardboard, we glued foam tiles sold as decorative background for terraria. These panels had a variety of surface textures simulating different types of natural rock. The tiles were fixed to the cardboard with 5 cm gaps between them. The gaps and the textured tile surfaces together produced complex, overlapping reflections.

The arena walls were light and could easily be repositioned so the arena layout could be reconfigured rapidly between data-collection runs. Sub-assemblies built from four panels fixed at right angles were also used as freestanding rectangular obstacles inside the arena. In addition to the panel walls and panel sub-assemblies, we used vertical poles as a second class of in-arena obstacle. Each pole was a wooden dowel of 25 mm diameter and approximately 60 cm height, held upright by a low-profile metal base so that it stood unaided on the floor. Poles could be placed anywhere within the arena. The arena floor consisted of interlocking high-density foam tiles with a smooth top surface, chosen to give the wheels reliable traction and to provide a uniform background contrast for the overhead tracker.

% Par 14
The robot (Fig. \ref{fig_robot}x) was a Pololu 3pi+~2040 (Pololu Robotics \& Electronics, Las Vegas, NV), a commercially available differential-drive platform built around an RP2040 microcontroller (diameter: 96 mm). We added a HiLetgo ESP8266 ES Wi-Fi module so that all higher-level control logic could run off-board on a host computer, while the robot executed low-level motion and acoustic commands on request. Communication with the host was by JSON-framed messages over a TCP link.

% Par 15
Three MaxBotix MB1360 XL-MaxSonar-AEL0 ultrasonic transducers (MaxBotix Inc., Brainerd, MN) were mounted on the robot in a 3D-printed housing. Two transducers acted as left and right ``ears'': their centers sat 37 mm apart and each was angled outward by 15$^{\circ}$ in azimuth, so that their main axes diverged by 30$^{\circ}$. The third transducer was mounted on the midline, 15 mm below the ears, and pointed straight ahead; it acted as the emitter, or ``mouth''. Emissions were tone bursts at a carrier frequency of 42 kHz and a duration of approximately 2.5 ms. Each ear recorded the resulting echoes as the demodulated envelope, sampled by the robot's analogue-to-digital converters at 10 kHz for 200 samples per ping, i.e., a 20 ms acquisition window per ear. An example measurement is plotted in Fig. \ref{fig_robot}x.

% Par 16
The robot's pose in the arena was read out from an overhead camera system. Two ceiling-mounted cameras covered complementary halves of the arena with a strip of overlap near the center. The robot carried a unique ArUco fiducial marker on its top plate, which the cameras detected frame by frame. Per-camera intrinsics were calibrated from images of a circular-dot calibration target; a homography was then fitted from a single image of a board placed on the floor, which fixed the world origin, the in-plane axis directions and the floor-plane pixel-to-millimeter scale for each camera. The two camera frames were aligned by measuring the in-plane offset between their floor-plane origins, so that one camera acted as the reference frame and the other's detections were translated into it by a fixed displacement. The output of this pipeline, queried at run time over a TCP link, was the robot's floor-plane position ($x$, $y$, in millimeters) and yaw angle, in a single shared coordinate frame across both cameras. The same coordinate frame was used by every component of the system, including the sonar-training labels, the trained policies, and the trajectory analyses presented later. The overhead camera system was also used to digitize the layout of the arena for each experiment.

\subsection{Training the inverse model}

\subsubsection{Acquiring and labeling the training data}

% Par 17
We trained the inverse model on echoes collected as the robot drove through the arena. Training data were collected in five sessions. For each session, the arena's overall outline was similar, as it was dictated by the field of view of the overhead camera system. However, by moving some walls and installing interior walls in the arena, the layout was altered locally. In addition, we placed a number of poles in the arenas at semi-random positions. This ensured that data were collected for a range of different arena geometries.

For each session, a predetermined number of semi-random positions within the arena were accessible to the robot (See Fig. \ref{fig:arena_layouts}xx for an example). We drove the robot to each position in succession using the overhead camera system. Once the robot arrived at a position, it rotated to five headings spaced $72^{\circ}$ apart. Modeling the emitter as a 15\,mm-diameter circular piston at 42\,kHz, its $-6$\,dB main lobe is about $\pm 22^{\circ}$ wide and at $\pm 35^{\circ}$ off axis its directivity has fallen by roughly 18\,dB. Because successive headings are $72^{\circ}$ apart, well beyond this main lobe, the five emissions sampled approximately non-overlapping, and therefore approximately independent, regions of the environment. At each assumed heading, the robot emitted one sonar pulse and recorded the resulting echo envelopes at the left and right ear.

Across the five sessions, we collected about 2100 binaural echoes. To place this into perspective, \citet{Barchi2013} flew \animal{ef} in an arena of about 6 by 3 meters. These animals emitted calls at a rate of 10-20 per second. At this rate, 2100 calls would take less than 4 minutes to collect, indicating that collecting the amount of data required to train the model would be trivial for bats.

For each robot position and orientation, we also collected a visual snapshot of the environment using the overhead camera system, recording its local geometry. We modeled our robotic bat with a visual field of $\pm35^{\circ}$ for two reasons. First, this coincides with the approximate width from which echoes from the walls and poles were received. Hence, we modeled a visual field matching the sonar system. Second, a field of this width is biologically plausible for a bat, lying between the binocular ($50^{\circ}$) and monocular ($120^{\circ}$) visual fields measured in the gleaning bat \animal{mc} \citep{Bell1986}. Within this visual field, we extracted the nearest reflector from manual annotations of the overhead view of the arena that marked the walls and locations of the poles. This nearest reflector set the ping's class label: \emph{wall}, \emph{pole}, or \emph{none}. The label \emph{none} indicated that no reflector fell within the cone, or when the nearest reflector lay beyond 1 m. For a pole, we recorded two quantities: its azimuth relative to the robot's heading, as a signed angle, and its range, measured to the pole's surface rather than to its center. For a wall, we recorded a coarse three-direction depth profile: the $\pm 35^{\circ}$ cone was divided into left, center, and right thirds, each about $23^{\circ}$ wide, and the nearest wall distance within each third gave that direction's depth, resulting in a very coarse depth profile.

\subsubsection{Inverse-model architecture and training}

% Par 20
The inverse model is a convolutional network (Fig.~\ref{fig:network}). A shared trunk of one-dimensional convolutions, pooling, and a fully connected layer turns each ear's envelope into a 32-number embedding, $z_L$ and $z_R$, one per ear. These embeddings are used by four output heads: a \emph{classification} head (wall, pole, or none), a \emph{depth} head (the three wall distances), an \emph{azimuth} head (the pole's azimuth), and a \emph{range} head (the pole's distance). The last three are regression heads and are heteroscedastic: each returns not only a value but also a variance, so every distance and bearing carries an estimate of its own uncertainty.

To a first approximation, the two ears can be considered as symmetric. This implies that swapping the echoes received by the ears should result in the same class, a depth profile with its left and right exchanged, an unchanged pole range, and a pole azimuth of opposite sign. Azimuth is thus the only output that the swap alters in sign, which is expected: it is the one quantity that must be read from a comparison between the two ears, whereas range is available from the time of return at either ear alone. We enforced the model to produce symmetric outputs as follows. First, the embeddings are combined into two 64-element vectors as follows,
%
\begin{equation}
  z_{LR} = [\,z_L,\, z_R\,], \qquad z_{RL} = [\,z_R,\, z_L\,],
\end{equation}
%
Each of the four heads is run twice, once using $z_{LR}$ as input and once using $z_{RL}$ as input. The output of both runs are then averaged after flipping the output of the depth head and the sign of the output of the azimuth head ran on $z_{RL}$. The class and range heads require no such correction, since their outputs are unchanged by the swap, and are averaged directly. The predicted variances are combined symmetrically, in the same way as the depth means. Encoding the symmetry this way makes the model respect it exactly, roughly doubles the effective training data since each emission also serves as its mirror image, and reduces the parameter count. This symmetry constraint is not strictly necessary; a variant without it, free to learn left-right asymmetries, fit the depth profile slightly worse (data not shown). Table~\ref{tab:inverse-params} lists the configuration.

The \emph{class} head averages its output over both orderings, giving a wall/pole/none prediction that is unchanged when the ears are swapped, and the \emph{pole-range} head is averaged in the same way, since a pole and its mirror image lie at the same distance. The \emph{pole-azimuth} mean is the only antisymmetric output, $\tfrac{1}{2}(z_{LR}-z_{RL})$, and so flips sign with the swap. The three regression heads are heteroscedastic: each returns a mean and a log-variance, $\log\sigma^2$, the latter expressing the model's per-estimate uncertainty.}
    \label{fig:network}
\end{figure}

% Par 21
The model was trained by minimizing a four-part objective: a cross-entropy classification loss plus three regression losses, one for the wall depth profile, one for the pole azimuth, and one for the pole range. The four terms carried equal weight. The classification loss used all echoes for each emission. The three regression losses were \emph{masked} and considered only for the relevant classes, so that the depth profile was fitted on wall echoes and the azimuth and range on pole echoes. All three regression losses were Gaussian negative log-likelihoods: each weights the prediction error by the model's predicted variance and penalizes it, so the network learns to report a calibrated per-estimate uncertainty rather than a point value alone.

We optimized with Adam (learning rate $10^{-3}$, batch size 64) for 60 epochs. For the first 20 epochs, the regression means were fitted with a plain squared-error loss before the variance terms were enabled, which stabilized early training. Three quantities were rescaled for training: the input envelopes were standardized, the pole-azimuth targets were divided by the cone half-angle, and the pole-range targets by the 1 m maximum labeled range, so that all regression targets lay on a common scale.

% Par 22
We fitted the inverse model on the data from the five sessions. From each session, we held out $15\%$ of the collected data in contiguous sets, i.e., the data collected nearest to a randomly chosen location. Training used early stopping on these regions, and we report performance on both the training data and the held-out regions (Table~\ref{tab:inverse-results}). Because the held-out regions are coherent patches of the arena, this check reflects locations the model was not trained on, and a small performance gap between the two indicates that the model has not overfit. This same model, trained on the $85\%$ that remained, is the one deployed on the robot. A single held-out region of this size is a noisy estimate, in particular for the pole class, which contributes only 90 of the 322 held-out echoes. Therefore, as a robustness check on the split rather than as a second model, we also cross-validated the same architecture and training procedure over the four spatial quadrants of the arena, so that every echo was scored once while held out. We report those figures alongside the deployed model where the two are informative together. Note that we did not collect or report on a validation sample. The aim of the model is to support the robot's behavior in the experiments reported below. Therefore, we consider successfully completing these tasks as validation of the model.

\subsection{Experiment 1: obstacle avoidance and target approach}

% Experiment 1 -- task
In the first experiment, we asked whether the noisy inverse model is sufficient to drive simple navigational behavior from sonar alone. The arena had a single pole. The robot’s task was to approach the pole while keeping clear of the walls. Hence, the task drew on the features the inverse recovers: the class of the nearest reflector, the pole's azimuth and range when a pole is nearest, and a coarse wall-distance profile otherwise. Moreover, the robot's task is analogous to a prey capture or foraging task where the bat is to approach an object at an unknown position, e.g., tethered insects \citep{Ghose2003}, mealworms \citep{Falk2014}, or artificial flowers \citep{Simon2011}.

As a comparison, in addition to trials in which the robot used sonar only, we ran trials where the robot used vision, as provided by the overhead camera system. Irrespective of which modality was used, at each step, the robot received a single local feature description. This is a modality-agnostic description of the nearest reflector within the robot's forward $\pm 35^{\circ}$ field. It consisted of the reflector's class and carried the matching geometry, i.e., the pole's azimuth and range for a pole and a three-direction depth profile for a wall. When no reflector fell within range, the feature was empty. When using sonar, the feature was the inverse model's output for the binaural emission acquired at the robot's current pose in the arena. When using vision, it was read from the overhead camera's digitized arena geometry at the robot's current pose, taking the nearest reflector in the same $\pm 35^{\circ}$ cone. Crucially, the vision condition supplied only this local feature, extracted from the robot's instantaneous pose, and never a map of the arena. Although the overhead camera holds the complete arena geometry, the controller could query it only within the same forward cone the sonar inverse operates on. Vision therefore stands in for a bat's local visual access to the scene ahead of it, rather than for a survey of the whole environment. Had vision supplied a map instead, the behavior it produced would have been generated by a map-using process, and the comparison between the conditions would no longer isolate the contribution of the inverse.

Note that this implies that perception was stroboscopic for both sonar and vision: the robot perceived the environment once per step. While sonar in bats is inherently a stroboscopic sense, vision is not. However, we modeled vision as intermittent to make the two conditions comparable in how often the world was sampled. They were not matched in how far the robot could see. The sonar inverse abstains once the nearest reflector lies beyond the 1 m range it was trained on, whereas vision reported the nearest reflector at whatever distance it stood, up to about 3.8 m in these runs. Across the ten vision runs the feature was never empty, while under sonar it was empty on two thirds of the steps. Vision is therefore an upper reference rather than a matched control, showing what the reactive rule achieves given accurate local perception and an unrestricted horizon. We return to this asymmetry when comparing the number of steps the two conditions required.

% Experiment 1 -- controller
The mapping from the local feature to the robot's motor commands was hand-coded rather than learned. In an animal this mappping is acquired through experience, as when bats are trained over repeated trials to fly to a perch or a landing target \citep{Holland2005,Finger2025}. We bypassed that acquisition deliberately, since the question this experiment addresses is not how such a sensorimotor association is learned, but whether the cross-modally trained inverse delivers features of sufficient fidelity for one to be built on at all. Therefore, the fixed rule stands in for the association a bat would acquire under reinforcement. Note that it holds no memory of earlier observations and no map of the arena. Consequently, nothing downstream of perception can compensate for a poor estimate, and whether the robot reaches the pole depends directly on the quality of the features the inverse recovers.

The way the robot used the extracted feature was programmed as follows. When the robot perceived no pole, and either no wall or a wall further away than $750$\,mm, it wandered through the arena with a slight curve, covering 150 mm between measurements. The curvature was re-randomized on each wall contact, so that repeated encounters did not send the robot along the same arc. In lab experiments, the bat \animal{ef} flies at about 2.5 m/s while emitting 10 to 20 calls per second \citep{Barchi2013}, and so covers roughly 125 to 250 mm between successive calls. The robot's 150 mm step falls within this range. Hence, while we did not aim to model a specific species, this indicates that the distance travelled between acquisitions is biologically plausible.

When the robot perceived a wall to be close by, it took evasive action. A wall within $750$\,mm triggered a billiard-like reflection, turning away by an angle derived from the three slice depths and driving on only while the wall was not immediately ahead; a wall persistently close in all three directions (a corner) triggered a large escape turn toward the more open side. Note that the robot kept the same 150 mm step near a wall as in open space. Bats do adjust their sampling to clutter, shortening their interpulse intervals from 40 to 100 ms in sparse clutter to 20 to 65 ms in dense clutter \citep{Petrites2009}. We varied the step only during the pole approach, so the robot samples a nearby wall less finely than a bat would.

When perceiving a \emph{pole}, the robot turned toward the reported azimuth, by a fixed fraction of the bearing capped at $25^{\circ}$ per step, and advanced a fixed step. When applied repeatedly, this curves the path onto the pole. The displacement between sensory acquisitions was reduced to 75 mm, to model the increased call rate and reduced flight speed of bats approaching a target. In \animal{ef}, the rate rises from 2 to 10 calls per second while searching to as much as 200 per second in the terminal buzz \citep{Ghose2006}. Either an increase in rate or a decrease in speed shortens the distance flown between calls, and halving the step models that shortening: at the cruising speed above, 75 mm corresponds to about 30 calls per second, between the search and buzz rates reported for this species. Practically, the smaller steps during the approach of the pole also resulted in the robot reporting the detection of the pole for more steps, enabling us to assess the stability of the detection. Table~\ref{tab:controller-params} lists every constant of the controller together with what it governs.

% Experiment 1 -- trial design
The experiment was a full factorial of five start poses and two pole placements, giving ten trials. The five starts were marked on the arena floor with tape and their coordinates recorded by driving the robot onto each mark and reading the overhead tracker, so that the start poses and the trajectories share one source of measurement error rather than introducing a second. The two pole placements were about $2$\,m apart and each was selected, before any robot run, from a simulated sweep over candidate positions spanning the arena interior, retaining positions that the reactive rule could reach from every start under perfect perception and that stood well clear of the walls. Each of the ten trials was run once under sonar and once under vision. Within a trial the two conditions shared a controller random seed, so the initial curl sign, its magnitude, and every subsequent re-roll were identical across the pair; any difference between the two runs of a trial is therefore attributable to the source of the local feature rather than to the controller having wandered differently. In nine of the ten trials the pole was geometrically out of reach of the senses at the start pose, lying either outside the $\pm 35^{\circ}$ cone or beyond the $1$\,m horizon, so the robot had to find the pole before it could approach it. We treat this visibility at the first step as a property to be reported for each trial rather than as a criterion for excluding the one trial in which the pole was visible, because geometric and perceptual accessibility do not coincide, and the cases in which they diverge are informative.

% Experiment 1 -- protocol
Each run began from its start pose and proceeded in discrete sense-act steps until one of four outcomes: the robot arrived at the pole, collided with a wall, became trapped in a corner, or reached a limit of $200$ steps. Arrival was judged on the robot's own perception, so a sonar run terminated on sonar and a vision run on vision. The alternative, stopping both conditions on the tracker's geometry, would allow a sonar run that never perceived the pole to drift within the stop radius and be credited with a success the inverse played no part in, an outcome that cannot arise in the vision condition, where the controller reads the same geometry as the referee. Arrival had three requirements. First, the perceived pole range had to fall below $400$\,mm. Second, the pole had to be perceived on three consecutive emissions, since roughly a quarter of the model's pole detections are spurious (Table~\ref{tab:inverse-results}) and a criterion resting on one emission would credit those as arrivals. The confirmations were collected by rotating in place rather than by driving, which costs no range and so does not consume the detection window. Third, the robot then regulated the perceived bearing toward zero, turning by a fixed fraction of the perceived bearing, re-sensing after each correction, and giving up after ten, which separates the outcome into arrival aligned and arrival unaligned. Nulling the bearing shows that the robot localized the pole and turned to face it, so an arrival cannot be earned by blundering into a radius.

We set the stop at $400$\,mm rather than at contact for two reasons. A threshold at contact lies inside the interval over which the emission and its echo overlap, where there is no echo to measure, and the inverse has no training data closer than $253$\,mm, because the acquisition planner held every waypoint $250$\,mm clear of all reflectors. Any range estimate below that distance is therefore an extrapolation, in exactly the regime the stop decision would occupy. In addition, pole recall itself falls as the robot closes, from about $82\%$ in the $400$ to $500$\,mm band to about $55\%$ between $200$ and $300$\,mm, so a lower threshold places the decision where the pole is hardest to see. A stop of $400$\,mm sits inside the well-sampled part of the training distribution and leaves several sense-act cycles between first detection and arrival. A collision was scored when the ground-truth wall clearance fell below $20$\,mm. Throughout, the overhead tracker served as an independent ground-truth referee: from the robot's true pose it computed the true class of the nearest reflector and the true pole and wall distances, none of which were visible to the controller and which we used only to score the run and to label each step as correctly or incorrectly perceived. The arena layout was one of those on which the deployed inverse had been trained, consistent with the specialized inverse this task requires (a point we return to in the Discussion).

% Experiment 1 -- measures
For every run we logged the full trajectory and, at each step, the perceived class and feature, the referee's ground-truth class and distances, and the action taken. From these logs we report, per condition, the proportion of trials ending in arrival aligned with the pole, the number of steps taken to reach it, the number of bearing corrections the terminal approach required, and the per-step agreement between the perceived and the true class of the nearest reflector. Note that the placement sweep described above also establishes what the rule achieves when perception is exact. Across $1400$ simulated rollouts, every candidate pole position was reached from every start, without a single collision or corner jam, and every failure was a timeout. It follows that a collision or a jam on the robot is attributable to perception or to drive error, and not to the rule itself.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Inverse model}

% Par 23
The model reached its lowest validation loss on the held-out region at epoch 43. The five sessions yielded 2135 labeled echoes ($53\%$ wall, $22\%$ pole, $25\%$ none), of which the spatial holdout set aside 322 ($15\%$). On the held-out region, the model achieved $80.1\%$ accuracy across the three classes, against $86.0\%$ on the training echoes (Table~\ref{tab:inverse-results}). Accuracy was uneven across classes. Walls, the majority and most distinctive class, were recovered well (held-out precision $88.6\%$, recall $89.8\%$), whereas poles and the \emph{none} class were harder (held-out recall $63.3\%$ and $81.2\%$). Because walls were classified with high accuracy, most remaining errors were confusion between poles and \emph{none} (Fig.~\ref{fig:inverse-results}A), which is expected since both correspond to the absence of a nearby wall and are separated only by a weak, often distant, pole echo.

% Par 24
The difference between the two columns is larger than the fold-to-fold variation of the same model, and should not be read as a measurement of how much the model loses off its training locations. Under the four-quadrant cross-validation described above, in which every echo is scored once while held out, classification accuracy was $83.9\% \pm 4.0\%$ and pole recall $71.2\% \pm 11.0\%$ (mean and standard deviation across folds). The deployed model's held-out column falls inside that spread. Note that its pole figures rest on 90 echoes, so a single echo moves recall by $1.1$ points. We report the single split because it belongs to the model actually deployed, but we treat the cross-validated figures as the better estimate of the model's accuracy.

% Par 25
When a reflector was present, the model also recovered its geometry, although coarsely. Pole range was recovered most accurately of the three geometric quantities. In the held-out regions its root-mean-square error was $99$\,mm (mean absolute error $72$\,mm, over $n=90$ held-out pole echoes), against $85$\,mm in-sample, and against $213$\,mm for a predictor that always returns the mean pole range (Fig.~\ref{fig:inverse-results}C). Under cross-validation the range error was $106 \pm 11$\,mm. Pole azimuth was recovered less precisely, with a held-out root-mean-square error of $12.9^{\circ}$ (mean absolute error $9.9^{\circ}$) across the $\pm 35^{\circ}$ field, against $10.6^{\circ}$ in-sample. This ordering is expected. Range follows from the time of return and is available at either ear alone, whereas azimuth must be recovered from a comparison between the two ears, a much weaker cue given the narrowband emission and the modest ear separation. The azimuth estimate is nevertheless enough to place a pole reliably on the correct side of the robot and to provide a usable, if rough, bearing. The wall depth profile was the coarsest of the three: held-out left, center, and right distances were recovered with root-mean-square errors of $339$, $391$, and $231$\,mm over the $1$\,m operating range (in-sample $307$, $300$, $273$\,mm). The corresponding mean absolute errors were smaller ($133$ to $212$\,mm), so the root-mean-square error is inflated by a minority of large errors rather than reflecting the typical case; the held-out center value rests on relatively few wall echoes and is noisier than the more even in-sample profile.

% Par 26
Because the three regression heads are heteroscedastic, every estimate is accompanied by a predicted uncertainty, and these tracked the realized errors. In the held-out regions the median predicted standard deviation was $\approx 11^{\circ}$ for pole azimuth, $\approx 86$\,mm for pole range, and $\approx 160$--$205$\,mm for the wall distances, comparable to the respective absolute errors in each case. The model therefore reports not only feature estimates but also a usable indication of how far to trust each one.

% Par 27
Taken together, these results show that the cross-modally trained inverse recovers the target features from single sonar emissions, but with substantial noise (Fig.~\ref{fig:inverse-results}): the nearest-reflector class is correct about $84\%$ of the time, pole range to within about a tenth of the operating range, pole bearing to roughly $11$ to $13^{\circ}$, and wall distances to within about a third of the operating range. Whether an inverse of this fidelity is nevertheless sufficient to support behavior is the question addressed in the following sections.

\section{Discussion}

% Terminology used throughout the Discussion:
%   cross-modal inverse training — the learning event: acquisition of
%     an inverse model in the receiver modality using feature labels
%     supplied by the supervisor modality. Conditions 1-3.
%   cross-modal direct learning — the agent learns or performs a task
%     in real time with the receiver modality engaging the environment,
%     relying on the cross-modally trained inverse. No internal model
%     of the environment required. Conditions 1-5 (= training + 4, 5).
%   cross-modal vicarious learning — direct learning's conditions plus
%     offline mental rehearsal of task behavior against an internal
%     representation. Conditions 1-7 (= direct + 6, 7).

% Par 28
For cross-modal direct learning of a given task to be observed in an experiment, several conditions need to be jointly satisfied. They fall into three tiers: the framework's mechanism, the experimental conditions for observing it, and the additional conditions specific to vicarious learning beyond plain cross-modal direct learning. The framework's central operation, cross-modal inverse training, requires:
%
\begin{itemize}
\item[1] A task-relevant feature must be physically encoded in the sensory data of both modalities and be recoverable in principle.
\item[2] One of the two modalities, the supervisor, must already extract this feature explicitly and represent it, so that it can supply training labels.
\item[3] The other modality, the receiver, must have acquired an inverse model that recovers the feature from its raw sensory data using the supervisor's labels. This acquisition can happen developmentally through natural bimodal experience or during the training phase of an experiment.
\end{itemize}

% Par 29
Cross-modal inverse training (conditions 1-3) is not in itself observable in an experiment. For cross-modal direct learning to be observed, two further conditions must be satisfied:
%
\begin{itemize}
\item[4] The task must hinge on the cross-modally accessible feature, not on some other feature that is accessible only in the supervisor modality.
\item[5] The animal must engage the receiver-side inverse at test rather than bypass it via a different strategy.
\end{itemize}

% Par 30
Cross-modal direct learning (conditions 1-5) is the observable deployment of a cross-modally trained inverse: the receiver modality engages the environment in real time, and the inverse makes the cross-modally accessible features available for task learning or performance. Cross-modal \emph{vicarious} learning of task behavior additionally requires:
%
\begin{itemize}
\item[6] The agent must hold an internal representation of the required task features. This representation can be modality-specific or amodal.
\item[7] The agent must engage in mental rehearsal of the task against this representation, generating imagined input for the receiver modality at arbitrary states of the world.
\end{itemize}

% Par 31
For bats, the inverse model for features encoded in both vision and sonar can be acquired at two distinct times, with different consequences for what an experiment can show. First, an inverse can be learned developmentally, through cumulative bimodal experience of natural environments. Bats often use sonar under conditions where vision is also available, and these instances provide the supervised cross-modal pairs the framework requires. The inverses formed this way are general and ecologically relevant. They cover features the animal regularly encounters across many environments, such as the presence and approximate distance of nearby obstacles, the coarse spatial layout of cluttered scenes, and the presence of familiar landmark types. Once developmentally acquired, these inverses are available to the bat in any subsequent task that probes the same features.

% Par 32
Alternatively, an inverse can be acquired during the experiment itself, when the task hinges on features that are specific to the experimental setup, idiosyncratic, or otherwise not covered by the bat's developmental inverses. Finding a particular perch placed in an unfamiliar room is one such case; discriminating between two laboratory-fabricated targets is another. For these tasks, the framework's conditions for cross-modal inverse training must be satisfied within the experiment itself. Vision and sonar must be co-available and consistently paired during the training phase so that vision's labels can supervise the sonar-side inverse. If the experimental design suppresses or limits this co-availability, the new inverse cannot form.

% Par 33
The two regimes carry different experimental signatures and call for different interpretations of negative results. Under the first regime, an experiment polls inverses the animal already possesses, and a failure to observe cross-modal performance suggests either that the animal did not deploy the inverse it has, or that the specific experimental setup falls outside the distribution the developmental inverse was trained on. Under the second regime, the experiment tests whether a new inverse can be acquired under the conditions provided, and a failure can reflect a violation of any of criteria 1 to 5. The bar an experiment must clear is therefore much higher under the second regime than under the first.

% Par 34
This distinction has a corollary for experimental design. The more ecologically remote the task, the more its features fall outside any developmentally-formed inverse, and the more the experiment must itself do the work of establishing the new inverse. Tasks built around laboratory-fabricated targets, geometric discriminations bats would never encounter in the wild, or arbitrary perch-in-room mappings all sit in the second regime by construction. The probability of observing cross-modal direct learning in such tasks decreases accordingly, not because cross-modal direct learning is absent in bats but because the experiment is asking the bat to acquire a new inverse from scratch under whatever cross-modal supervision the design provides. Ecologically natural tasks, by contrast, can lean on inverses the animal has already formed; the experiment then needs only to probe whether the animal deploys them.

% Par 35
The joint criteria are stringent. They are especially demanding in experiments where the bat has access to both modalities during training and is free to learn the task using any feature it finds, since the animal might converge on a strategy that does not engage the cross-modal route. Despite its potential adaptive value, convincing evidence for cross-modal direct learning in bats is lacking \citep[see also][]{Page2021}.

% Par 36
\citet{Danilovich2019} report two experiments testing cross-modal direct learning using \animal{ra}. In the first experiment, bats learned to discriminate two shapes while having access to both vision and sonar. Next, they tested whether the bats could discriminate the objects using sonar alone. They found that the bats were unable to discriminate between the objects in the dark.
This failure to demonstrate cross-modal direct learning might have been due to several criteria not being satisfied. First, with additional training in darkness, only one out of four bats was eventually able to complete the task using sonar. This indicates that the task might have been hard to complete acoustically, perhaps because task-relevant features cross-modally encoded at sufficient fidelity were not available (criterion 1), preventing the bats from learning or using an inverse model (criterion 3). In addition, during training with vision available, bats might have learned to use features of the objects that are not cross-modally available, leading to a failure during the testing phase when those features were removed (criterion 5).

% Par 37
In a second experiment, \citet{Danilovich2019} trained \animal{ra} to discriminate between two textures using sonar. Next, they tested whether the bats could perform the task using vision. In a cleaner replication of the experiment, only one out of five bats managed this \citep[Round 2 in][]{Danilovich2019}. This limited cross-modal performance could again be explained by a failure to satisfy the criteria outlined above. Even if bats were able to perceive task-relevant features using both sonar and vision (criteria 1-3), they might have learned to use these, except for the successful individual, during the training (criterion 5).

% Par 38
Similar limitations make findings of \citet{Holland2005} inconclusive. These authors trained \animal{ra} to find a hidden perch in light with sonar masked by ultrasonic noise, then tested in darkness with echolocation alone. Performance dropped and bats had to relearn finding the perch, which they were able to do. The authors interpreted the need to relearn as evidence of no transfer between the two sensory systems. However, as for the experiments reported by \citet{Danilovich2019}, experimental evidence supporting criterion 1 is lacking. More fundamentally, the masking-noise design invalidates criterion 3. With sonar suppressed during training, the supervised cross-modal pair the framework requires cannot form, and any task-specific inverse model for finding the perch in this room had no opportunity to be acquired. Pre-existing inverses developed through natural bimodal experience might have remained available, but they would not address the specific perch-in-this-room mapping the task probes.

% Par 39
Even positive evidence needs to be scrutinised. \citet{Finger2025} trained one group of \animal{ra} to land on a fixed perch in light and a second only in darkness. The light-trained group later landed in darkness, while the dark-only group failed across many trials, with three of four bats never contacting the perch at all. \citet{Finger2025} interpret these findings as evidence for some form of cross-modal direct learning since bats that were first trained using sonar and vision managed to later find the perch using sonar alone while the sonar-only group did not. However, the two of three pretrained bats required substantial additional training when restricted to only using sonar. Hence, in these animals cross-modal direct learning must have been limited.

%Sonar can support the classification of complex natural scenes and objects. Echolocating bats discriminate plant species and other extended targets from the statistics of their echoes \citep{Yovel2009,Yovel2011}, and biomimetic sonar systems reproduce comparable abilities: recognizing places from echo signatures \citep{Vanderelst2016}, detecting passageways in dense foliage \citep{Wang2022}, and retaining, under an efficient encoding, enough spectrotemporal information to match bats across a range of discrimination tasks \citep{Achutha2021}. In one such system, the labels are supplied cross-modally by a camera that teaches an echo classifier which views contain a passageway \citep{Wang2022}, the same supervisory arrangement we adopt here. These studies establish that task-relevant features are physically present in sonar echoes and can be extracted, in some cases under exactly the cross-modal supervision the framework requires. They stop short, however, of showing that such features are used to drive behavior. Where bat-like sonar has controlled an autonomous robot, the behavior relied on echo range and direction rather than on the learned classification, which was demonstrated separately \citep{Eliakim2018}. To the best of our knowledge, it has not been shown that sonar and vision together can be used to learn a feature-based inverse model whose output is sufficient to complete a spatial task.

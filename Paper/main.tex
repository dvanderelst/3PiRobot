\section{Introduction}

% Par 1
Sensory modalities (e.g., vision, olfaction, hearing, echolocation) differ greatly in what they allow an animal to perceive about its environment. Each modality encodes different environmental features \citep[e.g.,][]{Boonman2013}. Bat echolocation, for example, carries the distance to the nearest reflecting surface faithfully \citep{Kothari2018}, the spatial layout of complex scenes only lossily \citep{Vanderelst2016}, and colour not at all. Vision, in contrast, carries colour and the environment's two-dimensional layout faithfully.

Despite their differences, different modalities can carry information about the same feature. Their access to the world overlaps, allowing animals to encode world features through more than one sensory chain. Bottlenose dolphins, for example, recognise object features across echolocation and vision \citep{Herman1998,Harley2003,Bruck2022}. Rodents access spatial and shape features of nearby objects through both whiskers and vision \citep{Winters2010,Nikbakht2018,Guyoton2025}. Crucially, when modalities carry the same feature, they can differ in how accessible it is and how much computational effort is needed to recover and represent it. For example, for vision, the azimuth and elevation of objects is easility recoverable because of the retinoptopical encoding. Distance, on the other hand, is recoverable only approximately from cues such as disparity, motion parallax, and occlusion. Likewise, in hearing, the azimuth and elevation of a sound source requires computation to recover. 

The function that recovers a feature from a modality's sensory data is often called the modality's \emph{inverse model}. In animals, it is what the modality's neural pathways implement to turn sensation into perception and representation. The complexity of this inverse depends on the specific combination of modality and feature, not on the modality alone. For some combinations the inverse is trivial, or at least directly available from the modality's encoding. In bats, for instance, the auditory pathway and cortical circuits filter and transform raw echo data to explicitly represent the distance to the nearest object \citep{Kothari2018,Suga1990}. Similarly, vision's retinotopic organisation makes object azimuth directly readable from the location of activity in the visual pathway. For other combinations the inverse must be learned. The binaural signal carries source azimuth, but its extraction requires neural circuitry that compares interaural time and intensity differences \citep{Grothe2003}. Similarly, vision carries information about depth, but its extraction requires specialised circuitry that combines binocular disparity, motion parallax, and related cues \citep{CummingDeAngelis2001,Parker2007}. The same modality can therefore appear on both sides of the asymmetry, depending on which feature is considered.

The overlap, but asymmetry, in feature encoding across modalities gives rise to a learning opportunity. If one modality already extracts a given feature, it can supply training labels for another modality whose chain carries the same feature but does not yet extract and represent it. Barn owls are a well-known example. In these birds, vision calibrates the auditory map of space, and prisms that displace the visual map induce a matching displacement of the auditory map \citep{Knudsen1995}. The auditory pathway carries the spatial information but the inverse model is lacking. What vision supplies is the supervisory signal that labels auditory patterns with world locations, allowing the the auditory inverse model to be learned. Once trained, the auditory pathway can run the inverse model on its own mapping sound to space, and the owl can hunt in darkness. The same kind of cross-modal calibration is documented in mammals. The auditory space map in ferret superior colliculus develops normally only when matched visual experience is available \citep{KingCarlile1995}, and adult primate auditory localization can be recalibrated by sustained audio-visual spatial conflict \citep{Recanzone1998}. Interestingly, most documented cases of cross-modal calibration involve vision in the teaching role, perhaps reflecting vision's high spatial resolution and broad feature coverage \citep{MuganMacIver2020}. The framework for crossmodal learning of inverse modela, however, applies wherever the asymmetry of inverse difficulty holds.

Bats provide a particularly interesting case for cross-modal learning of inverse models. They have two sensory modalities, vision and echolocation, that both carry information about space but operate under sharply different environmental constraints \citep[e.g.,][]{Holland2005} [C: there must be more references for this very basic point.]. Echolocating bats operate in highly cluttered, spatially demanding environments: they navigate dense vegetation, return to specific roosts and feeding sites, and follow stable flight routes between them \citep{Holderied2006,Schnitzler2003,Barchi2013}. While echolocation is sometimes described as ``seeing with sound'' \citep[e.g.,][]{Moss2001,Simmons2012,Ulanovsky2008,Clare2015,Geipel2013}, it likely does not yield the rich, image-like information this metaphor implies. Indeed, for reasons we explained in detail elsewhere \citep[e.g.,][]{Vanderelst2015,Vanderelst2016,Steckel2013,Mansour2019,Mansour2019a}, sonar is poor at recovering the structure and layout of complex scenes. Natural objects return cascades of overlapping echoes \citep{Yovel2009,Vanderelst2016}, and the bat cochlea integrates these into responses that no longer resolve the individual reflectors \citep{Wiegrebe1996,Wiegrebe2008}. Behavioural and neural evidence both indicate that closely-spaced objects in cluttered scenes are perceived as single extended stimuli \citep{Geberl2019,Warnecke2018}. It follows that the information needed to reconstruct a 3D scene from sonar alone is, for natural environments, largely absent. Yet bats display remarkable spatial sonar-based competence, including stable idiosyncratic flight paths in dark cages \citep{Barchi2013}, centimetre-precision memory for feeder locations \citep{VonHelversen2005}, and successful homing after displacement \citep{Stones1969}.

Echolocating bats also see. Most echolocating species have functional vision \citep{Boonman2013,Hoffmann2019}, with behaviourally measured visual acuity in microchiropterans roughly comparable to that of similarly sized small mammals such as rats and deer mice \citep{ManskeSchmidt1976,BellFenton1986,Cechetto2020}. Their luminance sensitivity is within an order of magnitude of that of mice, sufficient for navigation under starlight \citep{Cechetto2023}. Therefore, the cross-modal calibration mechanism described above suggests how vision could supply spatial labels for learning an inverse model for specific features carried by but not easily extracted from sonar. A bat could learn this inverse model while having access to both vision and sonar, e.g., while exploring environments not in complete darkness. Indeed, many bats emerge from their roosts at dusk and forage under intermediate light levels of 1--10\,lux \citep{Boonman2013}, echolocate even in bright light \citep{Holland2005,Eitan2022}, modify their echolocation in response to available visual cues \citep{McGowanKloepper2020}, and preferentially recruit visual cues for prey detection when these are available \citep{EklofJones2003,Holland2009}.

The present paper instantiates this idea on a physical robot. The robot carries onboard sonar in a bat-inspired configuration (two receivers flanking an emitter) and is observed externally by an overhead camera paired with an annotated arena map. Distances to the nearest walls in three azimuthal slices are computable for any pose by direct geometric construction from the map; these geometric labels supervise the training of an inverse model from sonar envelope to the same three slice distances. The scene model also supplies imagined slice distances at arbitrary poses along a desired path, paired with the actions of a path-following teacher, to train a navigation policy. At deployment, the overhead camera and the map are out of the loop, and the robot follows a path in an arena it has only seen, not pinged.


\section{Discussion}

% Par 11
Despite the potental adaptive value of cross-modal vicarious learning for animals whose sensory modalities differ sharply in what they encode, to the best of our knowledge no published experiment has demonstrated it in bats. The three studies most directly relevant to the question, \citet{Holland2005}, \citet{DanilovichYovel2019}, and \citet{Finger2025}, all report little or no transfer from vision to echolocation in \emph{Rousettus aegyptiacus}. None, however, provides a competent test of what the framework actually requires.

% Par 12
\citet{Holland2005} trained bats to find a hidden perch in light with sonar masked by ultrasonic noise, then tested in darkness with echolocation alone. Performance dropped to chance, and the authors concluded that ``there is no transfer between the two systems''. However, the masking-noise design suppressed the supervised cross-modal pair the framework requires throughout the training phase, so the inverse from sonar to perch location had no opportunity to form. The authors themselves acknowledge that ``under light conditions, echolocation is available for at most a short time''. The result therefore probes whether transfer can occur in the absence of cross-modal supervision, not whether the framework's mechanism operates when its inputs are provided.

% Par 13
\citet{DanilovichYovel2019} and \citet{Finger2025} report a different kind of negative result with a shared confound. \citet{DanilovichYovel2019} trained bats bimodally on a shape discrimination (prism versus cylinder), then probed each modality alone. Vision alone was learned in a single session; echolocation alone produced failure in three of four bats across ten further sessions of reward. \citet{Finger2025} trained one group of bats to land on a fixed perch in light and a second only in darkness. The light-trained group later landed in darkness, while the dark-only group failed across four hundred and sixteen trials, with three of four bats never contacting the perch at all. In both designs, the echolocation-only condition itself fails. Hence the relevant features may not be reliably recoverable from echolocation at task-relevant precision in either setup, and the failure to transfer cannot be distinguished from the simpler reading that there is no sonar-side feature available for cross-modal supervision to deliver. This is exactly what the framework predicts when feature encoding capacity, not inverse-model learning, is the binding constraint, since cross-modal supervision can train an inverse for a feature only when the receiving modality physically carries the feature at sufficient fidelity.

% Par 14
A competent test of the framework's transfer prediction therefore requires three pieces. First, a feature demonstrably recoverable from sonar alone, evidenced by a sonar-naive cohort reaching criterion. Second, a training phase in which vision and echolocation are co-available without sabotage of either, so the supervised pair can form. Third, deployment under sonar alone, compared against the sonar-naive baseline on acquisition rate; the framework predicts faster acquisition for the bimodally-trained group. We are not aware that this experiment has been performed. The further prediction of full vicarious learning, that behaviour acquired through vision can be deployed through sonar via offline simulation from an internal scene representation, requires in addition that the animal engage in such simulation, and this last piece remains hypothetical for bats. The robot setup we report here instantiates the full architecture and provides an existence proof; whether bats deploy it remains open.

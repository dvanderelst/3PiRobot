\section{Introduction}

% Par 1
A sensory modality $\modality$ (e.g., vision, hearing, olfaction, echolocation) connects the agent to its environment through a chain of stages, each operating on the output of the one before it. The chain begins at a physical front-end \citep[e.g., visual pigments,][]{Cronin2022} and anatomical structures \citep[such as pinnae,][]{Obrist1993}, continues through neural circuits at every level \citep[from peripheral receptive fields to early feature detectors,][]{Kuffler1953,Franz2000}, including brainstem coincidence detection \citep{Grothe2003}, midbrain space maps \citep{Knudsen1995}, and cortical specialisations \citep[e.g., delay-tuned neurons encoding echo time-of-arrival in bats,][]{Suga1990}.

% Par 2
From an information-theoretic point of view \citep{Shannon1948,Pierce1980}, each stage along the chain is a channel, i.e., a process that takes an input signal (e.g., a spatiotemporal firing pattern in one population of neurons) and produces an output signal (e.g., a spatiotemporal firing pattern in a second population of neurons). A channel can preserve or discard (Shannon) information, but it can never create information not present in its input. Each stage, therefore, acts as an informational filter. The functional result is that all sensory systems are limited in what they perceive. Trichromatic vision is an obvious and familiar example: the photoreceptor mosaic collapses an entire light spectrum into three cone responses, so that distinct spectra producing the same triple of responses (metamers) are indistinguishable to any downstream processing. This is not a peculiarity of any particular sensory system but a general constraint on information processing, and it applies at every stage along every sensory chain. Stages of the chain are tuned to the animal's ecological niche, and in some cases, the tuning becomes extreme, producing so-called matched filters \citep{Warrant2016,Wehner1987}. We have described the chain in feed-forward terms for simplicity, but top-down signals such as attention, expectation, and recurrent feedback fall under the same constraint: they re-weight what is read out from current input but do not add new information about the world.

% Par 3
Information is indifferent to format. Only how much one signal tells us about another matters, not how that signal is represented. However, the representational format of information has computational consequences, and transforming its representation is a key function of a sensory chain. Therefore, unlike information itself, a representation is not format-agnostic: it makes some of what it carries explicit at the expense of the rest. It follows that two signals can be informationally equivalent, each recoverable from the other, yet not computationally equivalent. Because they use different representation schemes, one may make some piece of what it carries explicit and cheap to read, while the other leaves it implicit, recoverable only through costly further processing \citep{LarkinSimon1987}. For example, as \citet{Marr1982} pointed out, the same number written in Arabic and in Roman numerals is informationally equivalent, yet multiplication is cheap in one format and costly in the other. In addition to filtering, reformatting is the second purpose of the chain.

% Par 4
A \emph{feature} is a property of the world that some behaviour needs to know: the distance to a wall, the identity of a face, or the palatability of prey. Features are facts about the world, not representations of it; the agent has no direct access to them ($\feats$). What it has instead, at every point along a sensory chain, is a representation of those features: information about them, held in a particular format. The chain's two operations shape that representation. Filtering fixes how much information about each feature survives; reformatting fixes which features it makes explicit, in the sense that their values can be read off cheaply. Perception, on this view, is not access to the world's features but the construction of a representation ($\estfeats$) in which the features behaviour depends on are the ones made explicit. In a bat's auditory system, for instance, the distance to a reflecting object is already carried by the raw echo; the chain cannot create this information, so it is present even at the cochlea \citep{Wiegrebe2008}, and the chain's work is only to make it explicit, in the tuned response of neurons in the midbrain and cortex \citep{Kothari2018,Suga1990}.

% Par 5
The same chain supports more than one representation: which one the agent builds depends on what its behaviour requires. A representation taken close to the periphery is coarse, enough to detect motion; one taken from many recoded stages is deep enough to recognise a face. Behaviour-based robotic architectures \citep{Brooks1991} tap the chain early, world-model architectures \citep{Bekey2005} late; they differ in where they tap, not in kind. A chain can carry information about a feature without producing a representation that makes it explicit. The information is present; an explicit representation of it is not.

\subsection{Modalities preserve different features}

% Par 6
Sensory modalities are not interchangeable: their chains differ in which features of the world they carry, and how faithfully \citep[e.g.,][]{Boonman2013}. A chain may carry a feature faithfully, only lossily, or not at all. Bat echolocation carries the distance to the nearest reflecting surface faithfully \citep{Kothari2018}, the spatial layout of complex scenes only lossily \citep{Vanderelst2016}, and colour not at all. Vision has a different profile: it carries colour and the environment's two-dimensional layout faithfully, but distance only lossily, recoverable only approximately from cues such as disparity, motion parallax, and occlusion.

% Par 7
The features that modality $\modality$'s chain carries, fully or partially, form the set $F_\modality$. The features both modalities carry ($F_\MA \cap F_\MB$) are where the modalities can speak to each other. This overlap is generally non-empty. Bottlenose dolphins, for example, can recognise object features across echolocation and vision \citep{Herman1998,Harley2003}; for a review, see \citet{Bruck2022}. Comparable cross-modal access to spatial and shape features of nearby objects has been demonstrated in rodents using whiskers and vision \citep{Winters2010,Nikbakht2018,Guyoton2025}. Within the overlap, an agent can maintain a modality-invariant representation of a feature: a single internal estimate that does not depend on which chain supplied the data \citep{Guyoton2025}. For example, vision, whiskers and echolocation might all carry information about the shape or size of objects. Each modality also carries features the others do not have, and such a feature can be estimated only from the modality that carries it: vision can access features of space physically beyond the reach of whiskers.

% Par 8
This combination of asymmetric specialisation and partial overlap is what makes multi-modal sensing more than redundant duplication of single-modal sensing. Features unique to one modality supply information the others lack; features in the overlap are accessible through more than one channel, and that opens the door to cross-modal transfer.

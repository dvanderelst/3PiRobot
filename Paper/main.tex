\section{Introduction}

% Par 1
A sensory modality $\modality$ (e.g., vision, hearing, olfaction, echolocation) connects the agent to its environment through a chain of stages, each operating on the output of the one before it. The chain begins at a physical front-end \citep[e.g., visual pigments,][]{Cronin2022} and anatomical structures \citep[such as pinnae,][]{Obrist1993}, continues through neural circuits at every level \citep[from peripheral receptive fields to early feature detectors,][]{Kuffler1953,Franz2000}, including brainstem coincidence detection \citep{Grothe2003}, midbrain space maps \citep{Knudsen1995}, and cortical specialisations \citep[e.g., delay-tuned neurons encoding echo time-of-arrival in bats,][]{Suga1990}.

%this seems to be about active perception, let's keep this here for now but park it:, and feeds back out through behavioural processes \citep[such as eye movements,][]{Land2009} that themselves recode what is available to the next stage.

% Par 2
From an information-theoretic point of view \citep{Shannon1948,Pierce1980}, each stage along the chain is a channel, i.e., a process that takes an input signal (e.g., a spatiotemporal firing pattern in one population of neurons) and produces an output signal (e.g., a spatiotemporal firing pattern in a second population of neurons). A channel can preserve or discard (Shannon) information, but it can never create information not present in its input. Each stage, therefore, acts as an informational filter. The functional result is that all sensory systems are limited in what they perceive. Trichromatic vision is an obvious and familiar example: the photoreceptor mosaic collapses an entire light spectrum into three cone responses, so that distinct spectra producing the same triple of responses (metamers) are indistinguishable to any downstream processing. This is not a peculiarity of any particular sensory system but a general constraint on information processing, and it applies at every stage along every sensory chain. Stages of the chain are tuned to the animal's ecological niche, and in some cases, the tuning becomes extreme, producing so-called matched filters \citep{Warrant2016,Wehner1987}. We have described the chain in feed-forward terms for simplicity, but top-down signals such as attention, expectation, and recurrent feedback fall under the same constraint: they re-weight what is read out from current input but do not add new information about the world. 


%I parked this for now: The formal statement (the data-processing inequality) and its consequences for the framework outlined here are given in Section~\ref{sec:info}; we use it informally in what follows.

% Par 3
Information is indifferent to format. Only how much one signal tells us about another matters, not how that signal is represented. However, the representational format of information has computational consequences, and transforming its representation is a key function of a sensory chain. Therefore, unlike information itself, a representation is not format-agnostic: it makes some of what it carries explicit at the expense of the rest. It follows that two signals can be informationally equivalent, each recoverable from the other, yet not computationally equivalent. Because they use different representation schemes, one may make some piece of what it carries explicit and cheap to read, while the other leaves it implicit, recoverable only through costly further processing \citep{LarkinSimon1987}. For example, as \citet{Marr1982} pointed out, the same number written in Arabic and in Roman numerals is informationally equivalent, yet multiplication is cheap in one format and costly in the other. In addition to filtering, reformatting is the second purpose of the chain.

% Par 4
A \emph{feature} is a property of the world that some behaviour needs to know: the distance to a wall, the identity of a face, or the palatability of prey. Features are facts about the world, not representations of it; the agent has no direct access to them ($\feats$). What it has instead, at every point along a sensory chain, is a representation of those features: information about them, held in a particular format. The chain's two operations shape that representation. Filtering fixes how much information about each feature survives; reformatting fixes which features it makes explicit, in the sense that their values can be read off cheaply. Perception, on this view, is not access to the world's features but the construction of a representation ($\estfeats$) in which the features behaviour depends on are the ones made explicit. In a bat's auditory system, for instance, the distance to a reflecting object is already carried by the raw echo; the chain cannot create this information, so it is present even at the cochlea \citep{Wiegrebe2008}, and the chain's work is only to make it explicit, in the tuned response of neurons in the midbrain and cortex \citep{Kothari2018,Suga1990}.

% Par 5
The same chain supports more than one representation: which one the agent builds depends on what its behaviour requires. A representation taken close to the periphery is coarse, enough to detect motion; one taken from many recoded stages is deep enough to recognise a face. Behaviour-based robotic architectures \citep{Brooks1991} tap the chain early, world-model architectures \citep{Bekey2005} late; they differ in where they tap, not in kind. A chain can carry information about a feature without producing a representation that makes it explicit. The information is present; an explicit representation of it is not.

\subsection{Modalities preserve different features}

% Par 6
Sensory modalities are not interchangeable: their chains differ in which features of the world they carry, and how faithfully \citep[e.g.,][]{Boonman2013}. A chain may carry a feature faithfully, only lossily, or not at all. Bat echolocation carries the distance to the nearest reflecting surface faithfully \citep{Kothari2018}, the spatial layout of complex scenes only lossily \citep{Vanderelst2016}, and colour not at all. Vision has a different profile: it carries colour and the environment's two-dimensional layout faithfully, but distance only lossily, recoverable only approximately from cues such as disparity, motion parallax, and occlusion.

% Par 7
%Parked as this detail is not needed: In \citet{Herman1998} the dolphin matched novel objects across modalities at first trial, indicating a representation built from features accessible to both modalities rather than from learned object-specific associations.
The features that modality $\modality$'s chain carries, fully or partially, form the set $F_\modality$. The features both modalities carry ($F_\MA \cap F_\MB$) are where the modalities can speak to each other. This overlap is generally non-empty. Bottlenose dolphins, for example, can recognise object features across echolocation and vision \citep{Herman1998,Harley2003}; for a review, see \citet{Bruck2022}. Comparable cross-modal access to spatial and shape features of nearby objects has been demonstrated in rodents using whiskers and vision \citep{Winters2010,Nikbakht2018,Guyoton2025}. Within the overlap, an agent can maintain a modality-invariant representation of a feature: a single internal estimate that does not depend on which chain supplied the data \citep{Guyoton2025}. For example, vision, whiskers and echolocation might all carry information about the shape or size of objects. Each modality also carries features the others do not have, and such a feature can be estimated only from the modality that carries it: vision can access features of space physically beyond the reach of whiskers. 

% Par 8
This combination of asymmetric specialisation and partial overlap is what makes multi-modal sensing more than redundant duplication of single-modal sensing. Features unique to one modality supply information the others lack; features in the overlap are accessible through more than one channel, and that opens the door to cross-modal transfer.

\subsection{The forward--inverse model pair}

% Par 9
The operation described above, a chain that turns sensory data into a representation of features, is one half of a pair. Call it an \emph{inverse model}: a mapping from sensory data to an estimate of the state that produced it ($\moddata \to \estfeats$). Its converse, a \emph{forward model}, maps a hypothesised state to the sensory data that state would produce ($\estfeats \to \moddata$). The two run in opposite directions along the same channel: the inverse interprets data the world has already delivered, the forward predicts data the world has not. This pairing is not specific to cross-modal sensing. The same structure recurs, under different names, across the study of model-based agents: the inverse and forward models of a limb in sensorimotor control \citep{JordanRumelhart1992,WolpertKawato1998}, the world model or simulator used for planning in model-based reinforcement learning \citep{Sutton1990,HaSchmidhuber2018}, and the generative model whose inversion yields perception in predictive coding and active inference \citep{RaoBallard1999,Friston2010}. The cybernetic root is older still: any system that holds a goal against disturbance must contain a model of what it regulates, the formal content of the good-regulator theorem \citep{ConantAshby1970}. What changes between these traditions is only what is treated as the state: a motor command and the body's configuration, the latent dynamics of an environment, or, in the framework used here, a feature of the world ($\feats$).

% Par 10
The pairing is more than two directions of a single mapping; it is a coupled structure with a division of labour. The inverse grounds the agent in present sensory data: it answers what the world is, given what is currently sensed. The forward frees the agent from the present: given any hypothesised state, it predicts the data that state would produce, whether or not the agent has ever sensed it. Grounding and prediction are complementary, and together they are what makes off-line policy training possible. A behaviour can be rehearsed against states the agent has never encountered, using the forward model in place of the world, and then deployed on the inverse model, which grounds the same behaviour in the states the agent does encounter. Neither half supports this alone: the inverse without the forward can only react to what is sensed, while the forward without the inverse can only imagine, with no anchor in the present.

\subsection{Cross-modal learning lives in the overlap}

% Par 11
The partial overlap, together with the gap between having information and being able to read it out, sets up three learning regimes that this paper instantiates (Figure~\ref{fig:theory}). In particular, it facilitates learning of (1) inverse models and (2) forward models. In turn, these afford (3) cross-modal vicarious learning. We detail each below.

% Internally: green = Q2a (cross-modal inverse training); yellow = Q2b (forward model
% learning); blue = Q2c (vicarious rehearsal). Presented as claims, not as open questions,
% since the paper instantiates each one.

% Par 12
First, suppose both modalities $\MA$ and $\MB$ carry the same feature in their channels, but only $\MB$ has a readout $\estfeats_\MB$ for it. Then the readout from modality $\MB$ $\estfeats_\MB$ can supply training labels for $\MA$'s inverse model, a function from $\MA$'s sensory data to that same feature estimate. In barn owls, for example, the auditory map of space is calibrated during development by vision. Prisms that displace the visual map induce a matching displacement of the auditory map \citep{Knudsen1995}. In our framework's terms, hearing ($\MA$) and vision ($\MB$) both carry a spatial feature ($\feats$). Vision's readout ($\estfeats_\MB$) supplies training labels for hearing's inverse model; this does not add information to the auditory channel but specifies which function of its data the auditory readout ($\estfeats_\MA$) should compute. The supervisory link is needed only during training; at deployment, $\MA$ runs alone. This possibility is illustrated by the green pathway in Figure~\ref{fig:theory}.

% Par 13
Second, the same overlap supports learning a forward model: a mapping from feature estimates back to predicted sensory data. Given paired observations of feature estimates and sensory data ($\estfeats, \moddata$), the forward model can be fit directly. Continuing the barn owl example, the same paired exposures that calibrate the auditory readout in principle also support a forward map from estimated spatial position back to expected interaural cues, although this direction has not been directly tested in owls. In humans, the symmetric case has been directly observed: silent lip movements activate auditory cortex \citep{Calvert1997}, evidence of a forward map from visual speech features to expected auditory data, learned from paired audio-visual experience. The source of the feature estimates ($\estfeats$) during training matters: they can come from another modality, from an external map, or from the agent's own consensus estimate. What is learned is the channel's statistics: what modality $\MA$ would experience given any hypothesised state of the world. The yellow pathway in Figure~\ref{fig:theory} shows this regime.

% Par 14
Third, once a forward model of modality $\MA$ exists, it can stand in for $\MA$'s physical channel during training of any downstream behaviour. The agent can mentally rehearse what modality $\MA$ would tell it, without ever invoking the real channel. The proposal that off-line cognition runs on sensorimotor machinery decoupled from the world is well-developed in the embodied-cognition literature \citep{Wilson2002,Grush2004}; for an accessible recent synthesis spanning evolutionary biology, neuroscience, and artificial intelligence, see \citet{Bennett2023}. What the present framework adds is that the simulator is a forward model learned from paired data. At deployment, the real channel is restored in the loop; the rehearsal was used only to fit the policy. The blue pathway in Figure~\ref{fig:theory} illustrates this: a desired action paired with a hypothesised state drives a synthetic stream of sensory data ($\moddata$), which trains the readout-and-action chain that will later run on real data.

% Par 15
The rest of this paper is an empirical demonstration of all three regimes wired together, on a physical robot equipped with vision (overhead camera) and sonar. The robot learns a sonar readout from vision-supervised data (green), learns the forward direction from the same paired data (yellow), and uses the forward model to train a path-following policy entirely in mental rehearsal (blue). At deployment, only sonar runs in the loop. Learned world models of this kind have been identified as a central missing component of contemporary artificial intelligence \citep{Bennett2023}; the demonstration that follows is a concrete instance of cross-modal world-model learning in a low-information sensory channel.

\section{Information-theoretic statement}
\label{sec:info}

% Par 16
This section restates the framework above in formal terms. The narrative argument stands without it; readers untroubled by the filter/processing collapse can skip ahead.

% Par 17
Treat $\feats$, $\moddata$, and $\estfeats$ as random variables. The sensory chain of modality $\modality$ forms a Markov chain
\[
\feats \;\to\; \moddata \;\to\; \estfeats,
\]
meaning that $\estfeats$ is computed from $\moddata$ without any side channel back to $\feats$. Equivalently, $\feats$ and $\estfeats$ are conditionally independent given $\moddata$: $P(\estfeats \mid \moddata, \feats) = P(\estfeats \mid \moddata)$. From this structure follows the \emph{data-processing inequality},
\[
I(\feats;\estfeats) \;\le\; I(\feats;\moddata),
\]
where $I(\cdot\,;\cdot)$ is mutual information. The chain's information about the world is set at the front-end and can only stay the same or decrease at any downstream stage. This is the formal content of ``processing cannot create information''.

% Par 18
Per feature $f$, three regimes follow:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Preserved.} $I(f;\moddata) \approx H(f)$. The channel carries (almost) all of $f$; a readout is in principle straightforward.
\item \emph{Lossy.} $0 < I(f;\moddata) < H(f)$. The channel carries some of $f$ but not all; a readout exists, but it must be learned from the data's statistics.
\item \emph{Destroyed.} $I(f;\moddata) \approx 0$. The channel carries nothing about $f$; no readout can recover it.
\end{itemize}
We write $F_\modality$ for the set of features that fall in the preserved or lossy regime under modality $\modality$'s chain.

% Par 19
\paragraph{Cross-modal readout supervision (green).} If a feature $f$ lies in $F_\MA \cap F_\MB$ and modality $\MB$ has a readout $\estfeats_\MB$ of $f$, then a learnable readout $\inv_\MA : \moddata[\MA] \to \estfeats_\MA$ can be fit on paired training data using $\estfeats_\MB$ as the target. The data-processing inequality is unaffected by this supervision: $I(f;\moddata[\MA])$ is fixed by physics. What changes is whether the agent has parameterised a function of $\moddata[\MA]$ that approaches that bound.

% Par 20
\paragraph{Forward model (yellow).} A forward model $\fwd_\MA : \estfeats \to \moddata[\MA]$ is a learned approximation of the conditional distribution $P(\moddata[\MA] \mid \estfeats)$. Its fidelity is bounded by how well $\estfeats$ Markov-screens $\feats$ from $\moddata[\MA]$: if $\estfeats$ is a sufficient statistic of $\feats$ for $\moddata[\MA]$, then in principle the forward model can be exact in distribution. In practice $\estfeats$ is a lossy summary, and the forward model averages over the residual world variability.

% Par 21
\paragraph{Vicarious rehearsal (blue).} Given $\fwd_\MA$ and any source of $\estfeats$ (for example, a teacher policy operating on a hypothesised state), one can sample synthetic $\moddata[\MA]$ from $\fwd_\MA(\estfeats)$ and train downstream computations on those samples in place of real sensory data. The data-processing inequality still binds these samples: they cannot carry more information about $\feats$ than real $\moddata[\MA]$ would. The empirical question is whether the gap between the learned conditional and the true channel is small enough that the downstream computation transfers back to the real channel at deployment.

\section{Introduction}

% Par 1
Sensory modalities (e.g., vision, olfaction, hearing, echolocation) differ greatly in what they allow an animal to perceive about its environment. Each modality encodes different environmental features \citep[e.g.,][]{Boonman2013}. Bat echolocation, for example, carries the distance to the nearest reflecting surface faithfully \citep{Kothari2018}, the spatial layout of complex scenes only lossily \citep{Vanderelst2016}, and colour not at all. Vision, in contrast, carries colour and the environment's two-dimensional layout faithfully.

Despite their differences, different modalities can carry information about the same feature. Their access to the world overlaps, allowing animals to encode world features through more than one sensory chain. Bottlenose dolphins, for example, recognise object features across echolocation and vision \citep{Herman1998,Harley2003,Bruck2022}. Rodents access spatial and shape features of nearby objects through both whiskers and vision \citep{Winters2010,Nikbakht2018,Guyoton2025}. Crucially, when modalities carry the same feature, they can differ in how accessible it is and how much computational effort is needed to recover and represent it. For example, for vision, the azimuth and elevation of objects is easility recoverable because of the retinoptopical encoding. Distance, on the other hand, is recoverable only approximately from cues such as disparity, motion parallax, and occlusion. Likewise, in hearing, the azimuth and elevation of a sound source requires computation to recover. 

The function that recovers a feature from a modality's sensory data is often called the modality's \emph{inverse model}. In animals, it is what the modality's neural pathways implement to turn sensation into perception and representation. The complexity of this inverse depends on the specific combination of modality and feature, not on the modality alone. For some combinations the inverse is trivial, or at least directly available from the modality's encoding. In bats, for instance, the auditory pathway and cortical circuits filter and transform raw echo data to explicitly represent the distance to the nearest object \citep{Kothari2018,Suga1990}. Similarly, vision's retinotopic organisation makes object azimuth directly readable from the location of activity in the visual pathway. For other combinations the inverse must be learned. The binaural signal carries source azimuth, but its extraction requires neural circuitry that compares interaural time and intensity differences \citep{Grothe2003}. Similarly, vision carries information about depth, but its extraction requires specialised circuitry that combines binocular disparity, motion parallax, and related cues \citep{CummingDeAngelis2001,Parker2007}. The same modality can therefore appear on both sides of the asymmetry, depending on which feature is considered.

The overlap, but asymmetry, in feature encoding across modalities gives rise to a learning opportunity. If one modality already extracts a given feature, it can supply training labels for another modality whose chain carries the same feature but does not yet extract and represent it. Barn owls are a well-known example. In these birds, vision calibrates the auditory map of space, and prisms that displace the visual map induce a matching displacement of the auditory map \citep{Knudsen1995}. The auditory pathway carries the spatial information but the inverse model is lacking. What vision supplies is the supervisory signal that labels auditory patterns with world locations, allowing the the auditory inverse model to be learned. Once trained, the auditory pathway can run the inverse model on its own mapping sound to space, and the owl can hunt in darkness. The same kind of cross-modal calibration is documented in mammals. The auditory space map in ferret superior colliculus develops normally only when matched visual experience is available \citep{KingCarlile1995}, and adult primate auditory localization can be recalibrated by sustained audio-visual spatial conflict \citep{Recanzone1998}. Interestingly, most documented cases of cross-modal calibration involve vision in the teaching role, perhaps reflecting vision's high spatial resolution and broad feature coverage \citep{MuganMacIver2020}. The framework for crossmodal learning of inverse modela, however, applies wherever the asymmetry of inverse difficulty holds.

The opportunity for crossmodal learning may have particular relevance for bats. Echolocating bats operate in highly cluttered, spatially demanding environments: they navigate dense vegetation, return to specific roosts and feeding sites, and follow stable flight routes between them \citep{Holderied2006,Schnitzler2003,Barchi2013}. Yet for reasons we explained in detail elsewhere \citep[e.g.,][]{Vanderelst2015,Vanderelst2016,Steckel2013,Mansour2019,Mansour2019a}, sonar is poor at recovering the structure and layout of complex scenes. Natural objects return cascades of overlapping echoes \citep{Yovel2009,Vanderelst2016}, and the bat cochlea integrates these into responses that no longer resolve the individual reflectors \citep{Wiegrebe1996,Wiegrebe2008}. Behavioural and neural evidence both indicate that closely-spaced objects in cluttered scenes are perceived as single extended stimuli \citep{Geberl2019,Warnecke2018}. It follows that the information needed to reconstruct a 3D scene from sonar alone is, for natural environments, largely absent. Yet bats display remarkable spatial sonar-based competence, including stable idiosyncratic flight paths in dark cages \citep{Barchi2013}, centimetre-precision memory for feeder locations \citep{VonHelversen2005}, and successful homing after displacement \citep{Stones1969}.

A bat with a visual representation of an environment and a inverse model for feature available to both sonar and vision, could engage in offline rehearsal of behaviours that will later be deployed by sonar alone. In other words, an inverse model for features shared across sonar and vision could support cross-modal vicarious learning, i.e., training a behaviour through one modality and deploying it through another.

Cross-modal vicarious learning would specifically benefit sensory modality with low informational throughput that are ill-suited to build an coherent environmental representation such as whisking, sonar, or tactiale sensinging. The present paper instantiates this idea on a physical robot. The robot carries onboard sonar in a bat-inspired configuration (two receivers flanking an emitter) and is observed externally by an overhead camera paired with an annotated arena map. Distances to the nearest walls in three azimuthal slices are computable for any pose by direct geometric construction from the map; these geometric labels supervise the training of an inverse model from sonar envelope to the same three slice distances. The scene model also supplies imagined slice distances at arbitrary poses along a desired path, paired with the actions of a path-following teacher, to train a navigation policy. At deployment, the overhead camera and the map are out of the loop, and the robot follows a path in an arena it has only seen, not pinged.
